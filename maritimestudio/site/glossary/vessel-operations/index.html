Vessel Operations

Vessel operations terms explained: passage planning, under-keel clearance, tonnage, offshore vessel types (PSV, AHTS, MODU), pilotage, VTS and more.

A

AHTS — Anchor Handling Tug Supply vesselOPERATIONS
An offshore vessel designed to deploy, recover and relocate anchors and mooring systems for drilling rigs and floating installations, while also carrying deck cargo and supplies. AHTS vessels combine high bollard pull with dynamic positioning capability, making them among the most demanding platforms for DP operators.
AIS — Automatic Identification SystemOPERATIONS
A transponder system that automatically broadcasts a vessel's identity, position, course and speed to nearby ships and shore stations via VHF. SOLAS requires AIS on virtually all commercial vessels over 300 GT on international voyages, and it is a primary tool for collision avoidance and traffic monitoring.

B

Beaufort ScaleOPERATIONS
An empirical scale from 0 (calm) to 12 (hurricane) that relates wind speed to observed sea conditions. It remains a common shorthand in weather forecasts, passage planning and operational limits for marine activities.
Bollard PullOPERATIONS
The maximum pulling force a vessel can exert at zero speed, measured in tonnes during a certified bollard pull test. It is the headline capability figure for tugs and anchor handlers, and directly determines what towing and mooring operations a vessel can safely perform.

C

Charter PartyOPERATIONS
The contract between a shipowner and a charterer setting out the terms on which a vessel is hired — by voyage, by time period, or by bareboat/demise. Charter party clauses govern everything from laytime and off-hire to performance warranties, and shape the commercial pressure under which vessels operate.

D

Dead ReckoningOPERATIONS
Estimating a vessel's current position from a known starting point using course steered, speed and elapsed time, without external position fixes. It remains a fundamental navigation skill and a sanity check on electronic systems — and in DP, the control system performs an analogous model-based estimate whenever position references drop out.
Deadweight Tonnage (DWT)OPERATIONS
The total mass a ship can safely carry — cargo, fuel, fresh water, ballast, stores and crew — measured in tonnes. DWT describes carrying capacity, and should not be confused with gross tonnage, which measures internal volume.
Draught (Draft)OPERATIONS
The vertical distance from the waterline to the lowest point of a vessel's hull or appendages. Draught determines under-keel clearance, port access and loading condition, and is read from draught marks or sensors before critical passages.

E

ECDIS — Electronic Chart Display and Information SystemOPERATIONS
The type-approved electronic navigation system that displays official electronic charts integrated with GNSS position, radar overlay and route monitoring, legally replacing paper charts when operated with approved backup. Generic and type-specific ECDIS training is mandatory for watchkeepers under STCW.

F

FreeboardOPERATIONS
The vertical distance from the waterline to the freeboard deck, representing the vessel's reserve buoyancy. Minimum freeboard is fixed by the Load Line Convention and marked on the hull; loading beyond it is both unsafe and illegal.

G

GMDSS — Global Maritime Distress and Safety SystemOPERATIONS
The internationally coordinated system of satellite and radio communications that ensures a vessel in distress can always alert rescue services and receive maritime safety information. GMDSS equipment carriage depends on the vessel's trading area, and officers hold dedicated GMDSS certificates to operate it.
Gross Tonnage (GT)OPERATIONS
A dimensionless index of a ship's total enclosed volume, calculated under the 1969 Tonnage Convention. GT drives a surprising amount of regulation — manning scales, survey regimes, port dues and which conventions apply — which is why "over 500 GT" appears so often in maritime rules.

K

KnotOPERATIONS
One nautical mile per hour (1.852 km/h) — the standard unit of speed at sea and in aviation. The name survives from the days of measuring speed by counting knots in a log line paid out over a timed interval.

M

MODU — Mobile Offshore Drilling UnitOPERATIONS
Any vessel or platform capable of drilling offshore wells and moving between locations — jack-ups, semi-submersibles and drillships. DP-operated MODUs (typically DP class 3) are among the most demanding station-keeping applications, since loss of position with a riser connected has severe consequences.

O

OSV — Offshore Support VesselOPERATIONS
The family of vessels serving offshore energy operations: platform supply vessels, anchor handlers, subsea construction vessels, walk-to-work and service operation vessels for offshore wind, and more. Most modern OSVs are DP class 2, making the segment one of the largest employers of certified DPOs.

P

Passage PlanOPERATIONS
The documented berth-to-berth plan for a voyage, developed in four stages — appraisal, planning, execution and monitoring — covering routes, hazards, under-keel clearance, contingencies and reporting points. Passage planning is an STCW requirement and a recurring finding in both port state inspections and accident reports.
PilotageOPERATIONS
The conduct of a vessel through confined or hazardous waters with the assistance of a licensed local pilot, who advises the bridge team on local conditions while the master remains in command. Effective master–pilot exchange and bridge team integration are classic BRM topics.
PSV — Platform Supply VesselOPERATIONS
An offshore vessel purpose-built to carry cargo — deck cargo, fuel, water, drilling fluids and dry bulk — between shore bases and offshore installations. PSVs routinely hold station on DP within the 500-metre safety zone of platforms, one of the highest-frequency close-proximity DP operations in the industry.

R

ROV — Remotely Operated VehicleOPERATIONS
An unmanned, tethered underwater robot flown by pilots on the surface vessel, used for inspection, construction support and intervention. ROV operations from DP vessels create specific interface risks — umbilical management and vessel excursion limits — reflected in the ASOG for the task.

S

Shuttle TankerOPERATIONS
A specialised tanker that loads crude oil directly from offshore installations — via bow loading systems from FPSOs or offshore loading buoys — and shuttles it to shore terminals. Offshore loading is a precision DP operation, and shuttle tanker DPOs operate under some of the strictest competence and weather criteria in the industry.
Squat EffectOPERATIONS
The bodily sinkage and trim change a vessel experiences when moving through shallow water, caused by accelerated flow under the hull reducing pressure. Squat can significantly reduce under-keel clearance at speed and must be calculated in passage planning for shallow transits.

U

Under-Keel Clearance (UKC)OPERATIONS
The vertical distance between the lowest point of the hull and the seabed. Company and port UKC policies define minimum clearances that must account for tide, squat, heel and swell — and verifying UKC along the whole route is a core element of passage planning.

V

VDR — Voyage Data RecorderOPERATIONS
The ship's "black box", continuously recording bridge audio, radar images, position, heading and key sensor data in a protected capsule. VDR data is invaluable in incident investigation, and replaying it has become a powerful tool for structured onboard debriefing and training.
VTS — Vessel Traffic ServiceOPERATIONS
A shore-based service that monitors and manages ship traffic in ports and congested waterways using radar, AIS and radio, providing information, traffic organisation and navigational assistance. Participation is mandatory in designated VTS areas, with reporting procedures set out in the sailing directions.
