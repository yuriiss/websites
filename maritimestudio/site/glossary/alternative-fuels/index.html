Alternative Fuels

Marine decarbonisation terms explained: LNG, methanol, ammonia and hydrogen as fuel, FuelEU Maritime, EU ETS, CII, EEDI/EEXI, IGF Code, methane slip and more.

A

Alternative FuelsALT FUELS
The collective term for marine fuels that reduce greenhouse gas and pollutant emissions compared with conventional heavy fuel oil and marine gas oil — principally LNG, methanol, ammonia, hydrogen and biofuels. Fuel choice now shapes newbuild design, crew training and compliance strategy, driven by the IMO GHG framework, EU regulation and charterer demand for green tonnage.
Ammonia (NH₃) as Marine FuelALT FUELS
A zero-carbon fuel candidate that burns without emitting CO₂ and can be produced from renewable electricity ("green ammonia"). Its challenges are toxicity — even low concentrations are hazardous to crew — lower energy density than oil fuels, and the need for pilot fuel and NOₓ/N₂O control in engines. First ammonia dual-fuel engines and bunkering pilots are moving the concept from research into early commercial service, with dedicated crew safety training a prerequisite.
Ammonia Safety & Toxicity ZonesALT FUELS
Design and operational measures that manage the acute toxicity of ammonia when it is carried or burned as a marine fuel. Because NH₃ is harmful to humans at low airborne concentrations, ammonia-fuelled ship designs define hazardous and toxic zones around tanks, vent masts, bunker stations and fuel preparation rooms, supported by gas detection, water spray systems, double-walled piping and dedicated ventilation. Crew procedures, personal protective equipment and emergency response planning for ammonia release are central to class and flag approval of these vessels.

B

Battery-Electric & Hybrid PropulsionALT FUELS
Propulsion arrangements that use lithium-ion battery systems either as the sole energy source (fully electric) or in combination with combustion engines (hybrid). Batteries allow zero-emission operation on short routes and in port, enable peak shaving and spinning reserve on hybrid vessels, and can improve fuel efficiency of diesel-electric plants by letting engines run at optimal load. Norway's ferry sector has been an early large-scale adopter, and battery systems are increasingly combined with alternative fuels on offshore and coastal tonnage.
Bio-LNG (Liquefied Biomethane)ALT FUELS
Liquefied biomethane produced from organic waste streams such as manure, sewage or food waste, upgraded to natural gas quality and liquefied for use in LNG-fuelled ships. Chemically it is interchangeable with fossil LNG, so existing LNG engines, tanks and bunkering infrastructure can use it without modification. On a well-to-wake basis its greenhouse gas footprint can be far lower than fossil LNG, which makes it a practical compliance option under frameworks such as FuelEU Maritime, though available volumes are currently limited.
Biofuels (Marine)ALT FUELS
Drop-in fuels produced from biological feedstocks — most commonly FAME biodiesel and HVO (hydrotreated vegetable oil) — blended with or replacing conventional marine fuels. Their appeal is that existing engines and bunkering infrastructure can largely be used unchanged, making biofuel blends the fastest route to near-term greenhouse gas reductions while sustainability of feedstock and certification (well-to-wake accounting) remain the key questions.
Boil-Off Gas (BOG)ALT FUELS
The vapour continuously generated as cryogenic LNG (stored at about −162 °C) absorbs heat through tank insulation. Ships manage BOG by burning it in the engines, re-liquefying it, or — as a last resort — controlled venting via the gas combustion unit. BOG management is central to LNG fuel system design and to the gas-related training required under the IGF Code.
Bunker Delivery Note (BDN)ALT FUELS
The mandatory document issued to a ship on delivery of fuel, recording quantity, density, sulphur content and supplier details as required by MARPOL Annex VI. BDNs and the associated fuel samples must be retained on board for inspection and form the primary evidence that bunkered fuel complies with sulphur limits. As alternative fuels enter service, BDN information is also increasingly used to document fuel origin and emission factors for regimes such as the EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime.
BunkeringALT FUELS
Taking fuel on board — historically a routine oil transfer, now increasingly a specialised operation as LNG, methanol and ammonia enter service. Alternative-fuel bunkering introduces cryogenic or toxic transfer hazards, simultaneous-operations (SIMOPS) restrictions, dedicated safety zones and crew competence requirements, governed by standards such as the IGF Code and port-specific LNG bunkering rules.

C

Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII)ALT FUELS
The IMO's operational carbon-efficiency measure, rating ships annually from A to E based on CO₂ emitted per cargo capacity and distance sailed. Ships rated D for three consecutive years or E in any year must file a corrective action plan, making CII a direct commercial driver for slow steaming, hull and propeller optimisation, hybridisation and fuel switching.
Cryogenic StorageALT FUELS
Storage of fuels in liquefied form at very low temperatures, such as LNG at about −162 °C or liquid hydrogen at about −253 °C, to achieve a practical energy volume on board. Cryogenic tanks use heavy insulation and cold-resistant materials like stainless steel or nickel alloys, since ordinary carbon steel becomes brittle at these temperatures. Heat ingress inevitably vaporises some liquid as boil-off gas, so tank design, pressure control and gas management systems must be engineered together.

D

Drop-In FuelALT FUELS
A fuel that can replace a conventional marine fuel in existing engines, tanks and bunkering infrastructure without hardware modification. Examples include certain biodiesel blends (FAME), hydrotreated vegetable oil (HVO) and bio-LNG used in place of fossil LNG. Drop-in fuels let operators cut well-to-wake emissions on today's fleet, which is valuable because most ships trading in 2050 will have been built with conventional machinery.
Dual-Fuel EngineALT FUELS
An engine that can run on a gaseous or low-flashpoint fuel — LNG, methanol or ammonia — ignited by a small quantity of conventional pilot fuel, and switch back to pure diesel operation when needed. Dual-fuel technology de-risks the transition to alternative fuels by preserving full capability on conventional fuel wherever the new fuel is unavailable.

E

E-FuelsALT FUELS
Synthetic fuels, also called electrofuels or power-to-X fuels, produced from hydrogen made by electrolysis using renewable electricity, often combined with captured CO₂ or nitrogen. Marine-relevant examples include e-methanol, e-ammonia, e-methane and e-diesel. Their climate benefit depends entirely on the electricity source and, for carbon-containing e-fuels, on where the carbon comes from; they are widely seen as necessary for deep-sea decarbonisation but remain expensive and supply-constrained.
EEDI / EEXI — Energy Efficiency Design & Existing Ship IndexALT FUELS
The IMO's technical energy-efficiency benchmarks: EEDI sets a minimum design efficiency for newbuilds, while EEXI applies an equivalent one-time requirement to existing ships. Together with the operational CII rating, they form the regulatory efficiency framework pushing engine power limitation, hull optimisation and alternative fuels across the world fleet.
Emission Control Area (ECA)ALT FUELS
A sea area designated under MARPOL Annex VI where stricter limits on emissions of sulphur oxides, nitrogen oxides or both apply. In sulphur ECAs the fuel sulphur limit is 0.10%, compared with the 0.50% global cap, while NOx ECAs require Tier III engines on newer ships. Established ECAs include the Baltic Sea, the North Sea and the North American coasts, and the network continues to expand, directly shaping fuel choice and abatement strategies on affected trades.
Energy Density (of Fuels)ALT FUELS
The amount of energy a fuel contains per unit of mass (gravimetric) or volume (volumetric), a decisive factor in ship design and range. Conventional fuel oils are hard to beat: methanol carries roughly half the energy per volume of diesel oil, liquefied hydrogen roughly a quarter, and ammonia sits in between, before accounting for tank insulation and safety spacing. Lower energy density means larger tanks, lost cargo space or more frequent bunkering, which is why fuel choice is always weighed against a vessel's trading pattern.
EU ETS (Maritime)ALT FUELS
The extension of the European Union Emissions Trading System to shipping, which took effect in 2024. Companies must surrender emission allowances for CO₂ from voyages between EU/EEA ports and half of the emissions on voyages into or out of the EU/EEA, phased in at 40% of 2024 emissions, 70% for 2025 and 100% from 2026. The scheme puts a direct price on carbon for ships above 5,000 GT and strengthens the business case for efficiency measures and lower-carbon fuels on European trades.
Exhaust Gas Cleaning System (Scrubber)ALT FUELS
Equipment that washes sulphur oxides out of engine exhaust, allowing a ship to burn cheaper high-sulphur fuel while complying with the MARPOL global sulphur cap. Scrubbers come in open-loop, closed-loop and hybrid configurations; open-loop washwater discharge is banned in a growing list of ports and coastal states, keeping the technology's long-term role under debate.

F

FlashpointALT FUELS
The lowest temperature at which a fuel gives off enough vapour to ignite in air when exposed to an ignition source. SOLAS generally requires marine fuel oils to have a flashpoint above 60 °C, which conventional distillates and residual fuels meet. Fuels below that threshold, such as LNG, methanol and hydrogen, are classed as low-flashpoint fuels and may only be used under the additional safety arrangements of the IGF Code.
Fuel Cell (Marine)ALT FUELS
A device that converts fuel — typically hydrogen, or methanol/ammonia via reforming — directly into electricity through an electrochemical reaction, with no combustion and, for hydrogen, only water as exhaust. PEM fuel cells paired with batteries already power short-sea ferries and are being scaled toward offshore and coastal vessels, bringing new class rules and crew competence requirements with them.
Fuel Gas Supply System (FGSS)ALT FUELS
The system that conditions and delivers gaseous or low-flashpoint fuel from the storage tanks to the consumers on a gas-fuelled ship. It typically includes pumps or pressure build-up units, vaporisers, heaters, filters and a gas valve unit that controls pressure and temperature to the engine's specification. The FGSS also incorporates safety functions such as double-walled piping, automatic shutdown and inerting connections, and its operation is a core element of IGF Code training for crews.
FuelEU MaritimeALT FUELS
The EU regulation, applying from 2025, that caps the yearly average greenhouse gas intensity of energy used by ships calling at EU ports, with limits tightening in steps to 2050. Measured well-to-wake, it rewards alternative fuels and shore power (mandatory for containerships and cruise ships in major EU ports from 2030), and allows pooling of compliance across fleets — making fuel strategy a commercial optimisation problem, not just a technical one.

G

Global Warming Potential (GWP)ALT FUELS
A measure of how much heat a greenhouse gas traps in the atmosphere relative to CO₂ over a chosen time horizon, usually 100 years (GWP100) or 20 years (GWP20). Methane's GWP is roughly 28–30 over 100 years but around 80 over 20 years, which is why methane slip from LNG engines attracts so much scrutiny. GWP values are used to convert different emissions into CO₂-equivalents so that fuels and technologies can be compared on a common basis.
Green / Blue / Grey HydrogenALT FUELS
Colour labels describing how hydrogen is produced, and by extension its climate footprint. Grey hydrogen is made from natural gas by steam reforming with the CO₂ released to the atmosphere; blue hydrogen uses the same process but captures and stores most of the CO₂; green hydrogen is produced by electrolysis powered by renewable electricity. The same labels carry over to derived fuels such as ammonia and methanol, and they matter because a ship burning grey-hydrogen-based fuel achieves little or no well-to-wake emission reduction.
Green Shipping CorridorALT FUELS
A specific trade route — for example between two committed ports — where governments, ports, fuel suppliers and shipowners coordinate to make zero- or near-zero-emission shipping viable ahead of the global fleet. Corridors concentrate fuel availability, bunkering infrastructure, regulation and cargo demand in one place, acting as proving grounds for ammonia, methanol and hydrogen operations.

H

Heavy Fuel Oil (HFO)ALT FUELS
Residual fuel oil from the refining process, the traditional workhorse fuel of deep-sea shipping thanks to its low cost and high energy density. It is viscous and must be heated for storage and injection, and its high sulphur content means that since the IMO 2020 sulphur cap it may only be burned in ships fitted with exhaust gas cleaning systems. HFO serves as the emissions and cost baseline against which alternative fuels are usually compared.
Hydrogen as Marine FuelALT FUELS
The use of hydrogen (H₂) in fuel cells or internal combustion engines on board ships, producing no CO₂ at the point of use. Hydrogen is stored either compressed at high pressure or liquefied at about −253 °C, and its very low volumetric energy density currently limits it to short-sea applications such as ferries and offshore support vessels. It is a low-flashpoint fuel with a wide flammability range, so vessels using it fall under the IGF Code and require careful ventilation, gas detection and materials design; its climate benefit depends on whether the hydrogen is green, blue or grey.
Hydrogen EmbrittlementALT FUELS
A degradation mechanism in which hydrogen atoms diffuse into metals and reduce their ductility, making components prone to cracking under stress. It particularly affects high-strength steels used in tanks, piping, valves and fittings exposed to hydrogen, especially at high pressure. Designers of hydrogen-fuelled vessels mitigate it through material selection, such as suitable austenitic stainless steels, along with qualified welding procedures and inspection regimes.

I

IGF CodeALT FUELS
The International Code of Safety for Ships Using Gases or Other Low-Flashpoint Fuels, mandatory under SOLAS for vessels fuelled by gases or other low-flashpoint fuels such as LNG. It sets goal-based requirements for fuel containment, bunkering arrangements, machinery spaces, ventilation, gas detection and hazardous area classification. Crews on IGF ships must complete STCW basic or advanced IGF Code training depending on their duties, and fuels not yet covered in detail, such as ammonia and hydrogen, are approved through the code's alternative design route pending dedicated provisions.
IMO Data Collection System (DCS)ALT FUELS
The IMO's mandatory scheme under MARPOL Annex VI requiring ships of 5,000 GT and above to record and report annual fuel oil consumption, distance travelled and hours underway to their flag State. The verified data feeds an IMO database and underpins the calculation of each ship's Carbon Intensity Indicator rating. It operates alongside, but separately from, the EU's MRV regulation, so many ships report under both systems.
IMO GHG Strategy & Net-Zero FrameworkALT FUELS
The International Maritime Organization's roadmap for decarbonising shipping. The 2023 Revised GHG Strategy targets net-zero greenhouse gas emissions from international shipping by or around 2050, with indicative checkpoints for 2030 and 2040 and a goal of at least 5% zero or near-zero emission fuel uptake by 2030. To deliver it, the IMO developed the Net-Zero Framework, combining a global fuel intensity standard with a GHG pricing mechanism; it was approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025, but formal adoption was postponed at an extraordinary session in October 2025 and negotiations continue in 2026, so it is not yet in force.
Inerting & Nitrogen PurgingALT FUELS
Procedures that replace air or fuel vapour in tanks and piping with an inert gas, usually nitrogen, so that a flammable mixture cannot form. On gas-fuelled ships, fuel lines and bunkering connections are purged with nitrogen before and after transfer, and tanks are inerted before gassing-up or before entry for maintenance. Correct inerting practice is a core safety barrier under the IGF Code, and the displaced atmosphere also creates asphyxiation hazards that crews must manage.

L

LNG as Marine FuelALT FUELS
Liquefied natural gas, mainly methane cooled to about −162 °C, used as ship fuel in dual-fuel or pure gas engines. It is the most mature alternative fuel, with an established bunkering network and a large orderbook, and it virtually eliminates sulphur oxide and particulate emissions while cutting CO₂ at the funnel by roughly 20% compared with fuel oil. Its overall climate benefit is reduced by methane slip and upstream emissions, but LNG-fuelled ships can transition to bio-LNG or e-methane without engine changes.
Low-Flashpoint FuelALT FUELS
Any fuel with a flashpoint below the 60 °C threshold that SOLAS sets for conventional marine fuel oils. The category covers LNG, methanol, hydrogen, LPG and ethane, all of which form ignitable vapours at normal ambient temperatures. Ships burning such fuels must comply with the IGF Code, which imposes additional requirements on tank location, ventilation, gas detection and crew certification compared with a conventional oil-fuelled vessel.

M

Marine Gas Oil (MGO)ALT FUELS
A distillate marine fuel similar to diesel, with low viscosity and typically very low sulphur content, used without heating. MGO is the standard fuel for many smaller vessels and for operation in Emission Control Areas where the 0.10% sulphur limit applies, and it commonly serves as pilot fuel in dual-fuel engines. It is cleaner-burning but more expensive than residual fuels, and it remains a fossil fuel with a corresponding CO₂ footprint.
Membrane TankALT FUELS
A fuel or cargo containment system in which a thin metallic membrane, supported by insulation resting on the ship's inner hull, holds cryogenic liquid such as LNG. Membrane tanks use the hull shape efficiently and suit large volumes, which is why they dominate on LNG carriers and appear on large LNG-fuelled ships such as container vessels. They operate at near-atmospheric pressure, so boil-off gas must be actively managed, and sloshing loads in partially filled tanks are a key design consideration.
Methane SlipALT FUELS
Unburned methane that escapes through the exhaust of gas-fuelled engines, most notably low-pressure four-stroke dual-fuel designs, with smaller contributions from crankcase venting and fuel handling. Because methane is a far more potent greenhouse gas than CO₂, slip can erode much of the climate advantage LNG offers at the funnel. Engine makers have reduced slip substantially through combustion optimisation, and regulations such as FuelEU Maritime and the emerging IMO framework account for it by assessing emissions on a well-to-wake, CO₂-equivalent basis.
Methanol as Marine FuelALT FUELS
Methanol (CH₃OH) burned in dual-fuel engines, valued because it is liquid at ambient temperature and pressure and therefore needs only modest tank and piping modifications rather than cryogenic systems. It burns cleanly with very low sulphur and particulate emissions, and green e-methanol or biomethanol can cut well-to-wake greenhouse gas emissions substantially, which has driven a rapidly growing orderbook led by container shipping. As a toxic, low-flashpoint fuel it falls under the IGF Code framework via IMO interim guidelines, and its lower energy density requires roughly twice the tank volume of fuel oil for the same range.

N

NOx Tier III / SCRALT FUELS
Tier III is the strictest nitrogen oxide emission level under MARPOL Annex VI, applying to engines on ships constructed after the relevant dates when operating inside NOx Emission Control Areas such as the North Sea and Baltic. Most ships meet it using Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR), which injects a urea solution into the exhaust to convert NOx into nitrogen and water over a catalyst; exhaust gas recirculation is the main alternative. NOx compliance is separate from CO₂ regulation, and some alternative-fuel engines still need SCR to meet Tier III.

O

Onboard Carbon Capture (OCCS)ALT FUELS
Systems that capture CO₂ from a ship's exhaust, typically using amine-based solvent scrubbing, then liquefy and store it on board for discharge ashore. OCCS could allow ships burning conventional or transitional fuels to cut emissions without switching fuel, but it demands significant energy, space and weight, and it depends on port infrastructure to receive and permanently store the captured CO₂. Pilot installations are in service and regulatory frameworks for crediting captured CO₂ are still being developed.

P

Pilot FuelALT FUELS
A small quantity of easily ignitable liquid fuel, usually marine gas oil, injected into a dual-fuel engine cylinder to ignite the main charge of gas or low-flashpoint fuel, which will not reliably self-ignite under compression. Pilot fuel typically accounts for a few percent or less of total energy input in gas mode, though the share varies by engine design and load. Because pilot fuel is fossil diesel, it is included when calculating a dual-fuel ship's overall emissions and fuel intensity.
Poseidon PrinciplesALT FUELS
A framework through which ship finance banks measure and publicly disclose how well the vessels in their loan portfolios align with IMO decarbonisation trajectories. Signatory lenders collect fuel consumption data from borrowers, calculate portfolio climate alignment scores and report them annually. The principles channel pressure for decarbonisation through access to capital, and parallel frameworks exist for marine insurers and for charterers (the Sea Cargo Charter).

S

SEEMPALT FUELS
The Ship Energy Efficiency Management Plan, a mandatory ship-specific document under MARPOL Annex VI setting out how a vessel manages and improves its energy efficiency. Part I covers efficiency measures such as voyage optimisation and hull maintenance, Part II establishes the fuel data collection method for the IMO DCS, and Part III contains the ship's plan for achieving and maintaining its required Carbon Intensity Indicator rating. Ships rated D for three consecutive years or E in a single year must include corrective actions in a revised SEEMP.
Shore Power (Cold Ironing)ALT FUELS
Supplying a berthed ship with electricity from the shore grid so it can shut down its auxiliary engines, eliminating local emissions and noise in port. Also called cold ironing or onshore power supply (OPS), it requires standardised high-voltage connections on both ship and quay, and its climate benefit depends on the carbon intensity of the local grid. Regulation is accelerating uptake: EU rules will require container and passenger ships to use shore power in major EU ports from 2030, and Norwegian ports have invested heavily in OPS infrastructure.
Sulphur Cap (IMO 2020)ALT FUELS
The global limit on sulphur in marine fuel oil of 0.50% by mass, in force under MARPOL Annex VI since 1 January 2020, with a stricter 0.10% limit inside Emission Control Areas. Ships comply by burning very low sulphur fuel oil or distillates, or by fitting exhaust gas cleaning systems that allow continued use of high-sulphur fuel. The cap reshaped the bunker market almost overnight and remains the most sweeping fuel-quality regulation shipping has implemented; it targets air quality and health rather than greenhouse gases.

T

Tank-to-WakeALT FUELS
The portion of a fuel's lifecycle emissions released on board the ship, from the fuel tank through combustion or conversion to the propeller wash. It excludes upstream emissions from producing and transporting the fuel, which belong to the well-to-tank stage. A fuel can look attractive tank-to-wake yet perform poorly overall — hydrogen or ammonia made from fossil energy emits nothing at the funnel but carries a heavy upstream footprint — which is why regulators increasingly assess fuels well-to-wake.
Type C TankALT FUELS
An independent pressure-vessel tank, usually cylindrical or bilobe, used to store liquefied gas fuels such as LNG under the IGC and IGF Code tank classifications. Type C tanks can hold pressure build-up from boil-off gas for extended periods, simplifying gas management, and they need no secondary barrier, which makes them the most common choice for LNG-fuelled ships and smaller gas carriers. Their drawback is relatively poor use of hull volume compared with membrane or prismatic designs, limiting them to modest fuel capacities.

V

VLSFO (Very Low Sulphur Fuel Oil)ALT FUELS
A blended residual fuel with a maximum sulphur content of 0.50%, developed so ships could meet the IMO 2020 sulphur cap without scrubbers or a switch to distillates. VLSFO rapidly became the dominant deep-sea bunker fuel, but its blended nature means quality and stability can vary between suppliers, so compatibility testing between bunkerings is prudent. It complies with sulphur rules while remaining a fossil fuel, so it is a baseline rather than a decarbonisation measure.

W

Well-to-WakeALT FUELS
A lifecycle accounting boundary covering all greenhouse gas emissions of a marine fuel, from primary energy extraction and fuel production (well-to-tank) through onboard use (tank-to-wake). Well-to-wake assessment prevents emissions from simply being shifted upstream and is the basis on which FuelEU Maritime measures fuel GHG intensity, with the IMO's lifecycle assessment guidelines taking the same approach. Under this lens, the production pathway of a fuel — green versus grey hydrogen, biogenic versus fossil carbon — matters as much as what happens in the engine.
Wind-Assisted PropulsionALT FUELS
Technologies that harvest wind energy to supplement a ship's engines, including Flettner rotor sails, rigid wing sails, suction wings and kites. Depending on route, weather routing and installation size, they typically deliver fuel savings in the range of 5–20%, directly reducing emissions regardless of which fuel the ship burns. Wind assistance is recognised in efficiency frameworks such as EEDI/EEXI and FuelEU Maritime, and installations on tankers, bulkers and ro-ro vessels have grown steadily.

Z

Zero-Emission VesselALT FUELS
A ship that emits no greenhouse gases in operation, typically powered by batteries, hydrogen or other fuels used in ways that release no CO₂ on board. The term is often used loosely, so it matters whether a claim covers only tank-to-wake operation or the full well-to-wake lifecycle including fuel production. True zero- and near-zero-emission vessels are central to the IMO's 2050 ambition, and early examples — battery ferries, hydrogen ferries and small coastal tonnage — are concentrated in short-sea markets such as Norway.
