Autonomous Vessels

Autonomous shipping terms explained: MASS, IMO MASS Code, autonomy degrees, remote operations centres, sensor fusion, collision avoidance, ODD and more.

A

Automation vs Autonomy DistinctionAUTONOMY
The distinction between a system that executes pre-programmed functions under human supervision (automation) and one that can sense its environment, evaluate options and select actions to meet a goal (autonomy). An autopilot holding a set heading is automation; a system that re-plans its own route around traffic is exercising autonomy. The distinction matters in ship design and regulation because responsibility, approval requirements and operator training differ depending on how much decision-making authority the machine holds.
Autonomous Navigation System (ANS)AUTONOMY
The software stack that lets a vessel plan and execute its own voyage: sensor fusion builds a picture of surrounding traffic and hazards, a collision-avoidance engine evaluates COLREG-compliant manoeuvres, and a control layer commands propulsion and steering. ANS installations today typically operate under crew or remote supervision, with authority handed back to humans in defined conditions.
Autonomy Degrees (IMO Degrees One–Four)AUTONOMY
The IMO's four-step scale for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships: Degree One — crewed ship with automated processes and decision support; Degree Two — remotely controlled with seafarers on board; Degree Three — remotely controlled with no seafarers on board; Degree Four — fully autonomous, with the ship's systems making decisions themselves. The scale frames how rules, manning and liability apply as autonomy increases.
Autonomy Software AssuranceAUTONOMY
The body of evidence and processes used to demonstrate that autonomy software — including decision logic and machine-learning components — behaves safely and predictably across its intended operating conditions. Because software failures in an autonomous vessel cannot be caught by an onboard crew, assurance relies on structured development processes, extensive simulation, code and model verification, and independent review by classification societies or flag administrations. It is a central topic in the approval of MASS designs.

C

Collision Avoidance SystemAUTONOMY
An onboard system that detects other vessels and obstacles, assesses collision risk, and either advises on or automatically executes avoiding manoeuvres. In autonomous ships it combines data from radar, AIS, cameras and other sensors with decision algorithms that must produce manoeuvres consistent with the COLREGs. Its reliability under poor visibility, dense traffic and sensor degradation is one of the most heavily tested aspects of any MASS design.
COLREG Compliance for Autonomous ShipsAUTONOMY
The requirement that autonomous and remotely operated vessels manoeuvre in accordance with the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea, the same rules that govern crewed ships. This is technically demanding because the COLREGs were written for human judgement and include qualitative concepts such as "ordinary practice of seamen" and "ample time", which algorithms must interpret in ways other mariners find predictable. Demonstrating COLREG-compliant behaviour in mixed traffic is a core element of MASS trials and approvals.
Communication LatencyAUTONOMY
The time delay between a signal being sent and received over a communication link, for example between a vessel and a remote operations centre. Latency affects how quickly a remote operator perceives events on board and how quickly commands take effect, which constrains what tasks can safely be performed remotely. Satellite links typically introduce more latency than coastal 4G/5G coverage, so latency budgets are a key input when defining a remotely operated vessel's operating area and fallback behaviour.
Computer Vision (Maritime)AUTONOMY
The use of cameras and image-processing algorithms to let a vessel's systems recognise and track objects such as ships, buoys, kayaks, floating debris and shorelines. Computer vision complements radar and AIS by detecting small or non-transmitting targets and by classifying what a contact actually is. Maritime conditions — glare, rain, fog, darkness and sea clutter — make robust performance difficult, which is why vision is normally fused with other sensor types rather than used alone.
Connectivity RedundancyAUTONOMY
The provision of multiple independent communication links — for example satellite, 4G/5G and dedicated radio — so that a remotely supervised vessel does not depend on a single connection to shore. If the primary link degrades or fails, traffic switches to a backup link, and if all links are lost the vessel must transition to a predefined safe fallback behaviour. Redundancy requirements are typically derived from a risk assessment of the vessel's operating area and autonomy level.
Cyber Risk Management for Autonomous ShipsAUTONOMY
The identification, assessment and mitigation of cyber threats to autonomous and remotely operated vessels, whose navigation, machinery and communication systems are software-defined and connected to shore. Because control commands travel over networks, threats include spoofed sensor data, hijacked communication links and compromised remote operations centres. Mitigations include encrypted and authenticated links, network segregation, monitoring, and designing the vessel to enter a safe state if its systems detect tampering or lose trusted communication.

D

Digital TwinAUTONOMY
A continuously updated digital model of a vessel or system, fed by live sensor data, that mirrors the physical asset's condition and behaviour. Digital twins support performance optimisation, predictive maintenance and remote diagnostics, and provide the simulation backbone for testing autonomous functions and training crews on vessel-specific scenarios.
Dynamic Risk AssessmentAUTONOMY
A continuous, automated evaluation of operational risk during a voyage, updating as traffic, weather, system health and position change. In autonomous vessels, dynamic risk assessment allows the control system or remote operators to adapt speed, route or autonomy level to the current situation — for example slowing down when sensor confidence drops in fog. It contrasts with the static, pre-voyage risk assessments traditionally used in shipping.

E

E-NavigationAUTONOMY
An IMO-led concept for the harmonised collection, integration and exchange of maritime information by electronic means, on board and ashore, to improve berth-to-berth navigation and related services. E-navigation covers standardised data formats, digital route exchange, and improved communication between ships and shore services. It is an important enabler for autonomous shipping, since MASS depend on reliable, machine-readable navigational information and structured ship-to-shore data flows.

G

GeofencingAUTONOMY
The use of virtual geographic boundaries to constrain where a vessel may operate or what behaviour is permitted within a defined area. An autonomous vessel's control system can be configured to remain inside an approved operating area, avoid exclusion zones, or change speed and autonomy mode when crossing a boundary. Geofencing is commonly used in test areas and port approaches to keep trials within permitted waters.

H

Human-in-the-LoopAUTONOMY
A control arrangement in which a human must review or approve an automated system's decisions before they are executed. In maritime autonomy this typically means the system proposes a manoeuvre and an operator, on board or ashore, confirms it. Related concepts are human-on-the-loop, where the human supervises and can intervene but the system acts on its own, and human-out-of-the-loop, where the system operates without real-time human involvement. The chosen arrangement shapes operator workload, latency requirements and regulatory approval.

I

IMO MASS CodeAUTONOMY
The International Maritime Organization's goal-based instrument setting out safety requirements for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships, covering areas such as remote operation, autonomous functions, connectivity and the role of the human operator. The first, non-mandatory version of the Code was adopted at MSC 111 in 2026, with a voluntary experience-building phase beginning around July 2026. A mandatory MASS Code is intended to follow, expected around 2030–2032, informed by lessons from the voluntary phase.

L

LidarAUTONOMY
A sensing technology that measures distances by emitting laser pulses and timing their reflections, producing a precise three-dimensional point cloud of the surroundings. On autonomous vessels, lidar is used for close-range perception tasks such as docking, detecting small objects near the hull and mapping quaysides, where radar resolution is insufficient. Its range is shorter than radar and its performance degrades in heavy rain or fog, so it is typically one element of a fused sensor suite.

M

Machine Learning (Maritime Applications)AUTONOMY
The use of algorithms that learn patterns from data rather than following fully hand-written rules, applied in shipping to tasks such as object classification in camera images, trajectory prediction of nearby traffic, predictive maintenance and route optimisation. In autonomous vessels, machine learning most often appears inside the perception system. Because learned behaviour is difficult to specify exhaustively, its use in safety functions raises particular verification and assurance challenges that regulators and classification societies are actively addressing.
MASS — Maritime Autonomous Surface ShipAUTONOMY
The IMO's term for a surface ship that can operate with some degree of independence from human interaction, ranging from ships with automated decision support and crew on board to fully autonomous vessels. The IMO describes this range using four degrees of autonomy, and a single voyage may involve several degrees at different stages. MASS is the umbrella concept under which international regulation, including the IMO MASS Code, is being developed.
Minimum Risk Condition / FallbackAUTONOMY
A predefined state an autonomous vessel transitions to when it can no longer continue its planned operation safely — for example after losing critical sensors, propulsion redundancy or the link to its remote operations centre. Typical minimum risk conditions include stopping and holding position, anchoring, or proceeding slowly to a designated safe location. Defining credible fallback behaviours for every foreseeable failure, and proving the vessel can reach them, is a fundamental part of MASS safety design and approval.

N

Norwegian Test Areas for Autonomous ShipsAUTONOMY
Designated sea areas in Norway where autonomous vessel technology can be tested under agreed conditions with the support of the maritime authorities. The first, in Trondheimsfjorden, was established in 2016, and further fjord test beds followed, reflecting Norway's sheltered coastal waters, strong maritime cluster and cooperative regulators. These areas have hosted trials ranging from small research craft to commercial projects and have contributed operational experience feeding into international MASS regulation.

O

Operational Design Domain (ODD)AUTONOMY
The specific set of conditions under which an autonomous function is designed to work safely — including geographic area, weather and sea state limits, traffic density, visibility, and required connectivity. A vessel's autonomy is only valid inside its ODD; if conditions drift outside it, the system must hand control to a human or enter a minimum risk condition. Precisely defining and monitoring the ODD is central to approving and operating autonomous vessels.

P

Path PlanningAUTONOMY
The process by which an autonomous vessel computes a safe, efficient trajectory from its current position toward its destination, taking into account charted depths, traffic, weather, vessel manoeuvring characteristics and the COLREGs. Path planning typically works on two levels: voyage-level route planning done in advance, and local re-planning that continuously adjusts the track to avoid other vessels and obstacles. The output feeds the vessel's track control and collision avoidance functions.
Perception SystemAUTONOMY
The collection of sensors and processing software that builds an autonomous vessel's model of its surroundings — detecting, classifying and tracking vessels, obstacles, navigation marks and the shoreline. A typical maritime perception system fuses radar, AIS, optical and infrared cameras, lidar and GNSS positioning. Its output, often called a world model, is what the vessel's decision-making functions act on, so perception reliability directly bounds how much autonomy can safely be granted.
Periodically Unattended BridgeAUTONOMY
An operating concept in which a crewed vessel's bridge is left without a watchkeeper for defined periods, with automated navigation and lookout systems maintaining the watch and alerting crew when human intervention is needed. It is seen as a practical intermediate step toward higher autonomy, allowing reduced bridge manning at night or in open water while crew remain on board. Approval depends on demonstrating that the automated watch performs at least as well as a human lookout within the permitted conditions.
Periodically Unattended Engine RoomAUTONOMY
An operating concept in which the machinery spaces run without continuous local supervision for defined periods, relying on automation, condition monitoring and alarm systems, with engineers on call. Long established in conventional shipping through unattended machinery space (UMS) class notations, it takes on new significance for autonomous vessels, where machinery may need to operate for entire voyages with no one on board. This pushes requirements for machinery redundancy, remote diagnostics and automated response to faults.

R

Remote Operations Centre (ROC)AUTONOMY
A shore-based facility from which operators monitor, supervise and when necessary control one or more vessels via communication links. A ROC typically presents sensor feeds, navigation displays, system health data and alarm handling, and may allow direct remote manoeuvring. Its design raises questions familiar from bridge design — workload, alertness, handover procedures and how many vessels one operator can safely oversee — and it is a central element of the operational concepts addressed by the IMO MASS Code.
Remote Operator Competence & TrainingAUTONOMY
The knowledge, skills and certification needed by personnel who supervise or control vessels from shore. Remote operators need traditional seamanship and COLREG understanding, plus new competences: interpreting sensor and system-health data, managing degraded communication links, supervising several vessels, and taking over control safely mid-situation. Formal international competence standards are still developing alongside the MASS regulatory framework, and simulator-based training is expected to play a major role, much as it does for bridge and DP operators today.

S

Safe StateAUTONOMY
A condition in which a vessel poses minimal risk to people, the environment and other traffic, which an autonomous system must be able to reach and maintain after a failure. Depending on the vessel and location, a safe state may mean drifting clear of traffic with lights and signals active, holding position on thrusters, or being anchored. The concept underpins fallback design: every identified failure scenario should have a defined path to a safe state that does not rely on onboard crew.
Sea Traffic ManagementAUTONOMY
The coordinated organisation of vessel traffic through shared voyage information, route exchange and shore-based services, extending the role of traditional vessel traffic services (VTS). By letting ships and shore centres exchange intended routes and timing digitally, sea traffic management improves predictability in congested waters. For autonomous vessels it offers a structured, machine-readable traffic picture, which is easier for autonomy systems to use than informal VHF coordination between human watchkeepers.
Sensor FusionAUTONOMY
The combining of data from multiple sensor types — such as radar, AIS, optical and infrared cameras, lidar and GNSS — into a single consistent picture of the vessel's surroundings. Fusion exploits the complementary strengths of each sensor: radar works in fog, cameras classify targets, AIS identifies transmitting ships. It also provides cross-checking, so that a false or failed reading from one sensor can be detected against the others, which is essential for trusting an autonomous vessel's world model.
Shore-Based MonitoringAUTONOMY
Continuous observation of a vessel's navigation, machinery and system status from a facility ashore, without necessarily exercising direct control. Monitoring is the lightest form of shore involvement in vessel operation: operators watch health data and alarms and can escalate to advisory contact or remote control if something develops. It is used both for autonomous vessels and, increasingly, for conventional ships as a fleet-support function, and it forms the baseline activity of a remote operations centre.
Situational AwarenessAUTONOMY
The perception of what is happening around a vessel, the understanding of what it means, and the projection of how the situation will develop — classically described as three levels applied to a navigator's watch. In autonomous shipping the term covers both the machine's situational awareness, built by its perception system, and the remote operator's awareness, which must be maintained through displays and data links rather than direct senses. Degraded operator awareness during remote supervision is a recognised human-factors risk.
Situational Awareness Camera ArrayAUTONOMY
A set of cameras mounted to give full or near-360-degree visual coverage around a vessel, feeding both machine perception and remote operator displays. Arrays typically combine daylight cameras with infrared or thermal cameras so that small craft, persons in the water and unlit objects can be detected in darkness and poor visibility. Stitched panoramic views from such arrays are a primary way remote operators regain the out-of-window view they lose by not being on board.

T

Teleoperation / Remote ControlAUTONOMY
Direct human control of a vessel's steering, propulsion and other functions from a location off the ship, usually a remote operations centre, over a communication link. Teleoperation corresponds to the IMO's remotely controlled degrees of autonomy and is distinct from autonomous operation, where the vessel's own systems make decisions. Its safety depends heavily on link quality: latency, bandwidth and reliability determine how faithfully the operator perceives the situation and how promptly commands reach the vessel.

U

Uncrewed Surface Vessel (USV)AUTONOMY
A surface craft designed to operate with no one on board, controlled remotely, operating autonomously, or combining both. USVs range from small survey and inspection craft to larger vessels for offshore support, ocean data collection and defence tasks. Because they carry no crew, their design can dispense with accommodation and life-saving arrangements, but they depend entirely on their perception systems, communication links and fallback logic — and, lacking an onboard lookout, they face the same COLREG-compliance questions as larger MASS.

V

Verification & Validation of Autonomous FunctionsAUTONOMY
The paired activities of confirming that an autonomous function is built correctly to its specification (verification) and that it actually behaves safely in its intended operating conditions (validation). Because real-world sea trials cannot cover every traffic and weather combination, validation relies heavily on simulation-based testing across large scenario libraries, complemented by controlled trials in designated test areas. Classification societies and flag states use the resulting evidence when approving autonomous vessels and their operating envelopes.

Y

Yara BirkelandAUTONOMY
A Norwegian battery-electric container ship developed by fertiliser producer Yara with Kongsberg, and operated with the involvement of Massterly, carrying cargo on a short coastal route in southern Norway. Conceived to replace truck transport between Yara's plant and nearby ports, the vessel has operated with a progressively reduced crew as technology and procedures are proven, serving as a testbed for autonomous coastal shipping. It is one of the most cited real-world reference projects in the international MASS debate.
