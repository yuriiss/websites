Energy Storage (ESS/EMS)

Marine battery and hybrid power terms explained: energy storage systems, battery management, thermal runaway, state of charge, spinning reserve, DC grids and more.

ABCDEFHILMOPSTUVZ

A

Active Front End (AFE)ESS/EMS
A power converter that uses actively controlled semiconductor switches (typically IGBTs) instead of a passive diode rectifier at the grid-facing side of a drive or battery converter. On ships, AFE converters allow power to flow in both directions, keep harmonic distortion low without bulky filters, and enable a battery to both charge from and discharge into the switchboard. They are a key building block of hybrid AC systems and onboard DC grids on OSVs and ferries.

B

Battery Cell, Module & Rack HierarchyESS/EMS
The physical build-up of a marine battery system: individual electrochemical cells are grouped into modules, modules are stacked into racks (strings), and racks are combined into the complete shipboard ESS. Each level adds its own monitoring, fusing and disconnection functions, so a fault in one module or rack can be isolated without losing the whole system. Class rules and system design typically require that the vessel can tolerate the loss of one string while still meeting its safety functions.
Battery Class NotationESS/EMS
An optional class notation confirming that a vessel's battery installation meets a classification society's rules for safety and, where applicable, for using the battery as a source of propulsion power. DNV, for example, distinguishes between Battery(Safety), covering installations where the battery is a supplementary source, and Battery(Power), where the battery is relied on as main or redundant power. The notations bring requirements for dedicated battery spaces, fire suppression, off-gas detection, ventilation and system testing.
Battery DegradationESS/EMS
The gradual, irreversible loss of a battery's capacity and increase in its internal resistance over time and use. Degradation is driven by cycling depth, charge/discharge rates, temperature and time at high state of charge, all of which the BMS and EMS try to manage. For a ferry or hybrid OSV it determines how many years the installed battery can still deliver its contracted crossing or spinning-reserve duty, and it is the basis for end-of-life and replacement planning.
Battery Management System (BMS)ESS/EMS
The dedicated control and protection system supervising a battery installation cell by cell — monitoring voltage, current and temperature, balancing cells, and disconnecting the battery when limits are exceeded. The BMS is the primary safeguard against thermal runaway and the source of the state-of-charge and state-of-health data the vessel's EMS relies on.
Battery Space Fire SafetyESS/EMS
The set of design measures protecting the dedicated compartment where a marine lithium-ion battery is installed. Class rules require batteries above small sizes to be placed in their own space with structural fire protection, a fixed fire-extinguishing system, off-gas detection and dedicated ventilation arranged to vent gases away from accommodation and machinery air intakes. The goal is to contain a thermal event to the battery space and protect the crew and the rest of the power plant.
Battery-Hybrid PropulsionESS/EMS
A power plant that combines combustion engines with a battery energy storage system, letting the vessel run engines at optimal load, absorb load transients from the battery, and operate short periods on battery alone. On OSVs and ferries, hybridisation cuts fuel consumption, running hours and emissions — and in DP operation the ESS can act as instantly available spinning reserve.
Blackout Prevention & Recovery (Battery)ESS/EMS
The use of an ESS to instantly pick up load when a generator trips, preventing the frequency and voltage collapse that would otherwise black out the switchboard. Because a battery responds in milliseconds, far faster than a standby diesel can start and synchronise, it can carry the vessel through the disturbance or, after a blackout, re-energise the switchboard and support restart of the generators. On DP vessels this capability directly supports station-keeping integrity and is a core argument for battery hybridisation.

C

C-RateESS/EMS
A measure of how fast a battery is charged or discharged relative to its capacity: 1C empties (or fills) the battery in one hour, 2C in half an hour. Marine ESS applications differ widely — spinning reserve demands short bursts at high C-rates, while zero-emission sailing draws lower rates for longer — and the chosen duty profile drives battery sizing, cooling and lifetime.
Capacity FadeESS/EMS
The specific component of battery degradation describing the reduction of usable energy capacity, usually expressed as a percentage of the original rated capacity. A marine battery is commonly considered to have reached end of life for its original duty when capacity has faded to around 70–80 percent of new. Vessel operators track capacity fade through the BMS and periodic capacity tests so that route energy budgets, DP reserve calculations and class documentation remain valid.
Cell Balancing (Equalisation)ESS/EMS
The process of evening out small state-of-charge differences between the series-connected cells in a battery string. Without balancing, the weakest cell limits the whole string, reaching its voltage limits first and forcing the system to stop charging or discharging early. The BMS performs balancing either passively, by bleeding energy from the highest cells through resistors, or actively, by shuttling energy between cells, preserving usable capacity and cell health over the system's life.
Closed-Loop Liquid CoolingESS/EMS
A thermal management arrangement in which battery modules are cooled by a sealed circuit of water-glycol coolant, with heat rejected to the ship's central fresh-water or seawater cooling system. Liquid cooling keeps cell temperatures within a narrow band even at high charge and discharge rates, which is essential for cycle life and for the fast charging used by battery ferries. It also decouples the battery space climate from the cells themselves, complementing the room's HVAC and ventilation functions.
Cycle LifeESS/EMS
The number of complete charge-discharge cycles a battery can deliver before its capacity fades to a defined end-of-life threshold, typically 70–80 percent of rated capacity. Cycle life depends strongly on depth of discharge, temperature and C-rate, so the same cells last far longer in a gentle spinning-reserve role than in heavy daily ferry cycling. Battery suppliers state cycle life under defined test conditions, and the EMS operating strategy is tuned to meet the vessel's required service years.

D

DC Grid (Direct Current Distribution)ESS/EMS
A power distribution architecture in which generators, batteries and consumers connect to a common DC bus instead of a fixed-frequency AC switchboard. DC grids let engines run at variable speed for better fuel efficiency, integrate batteries and fuel cells naturally, and reduce switchboard size — which is why they dominate modern hybrid OSV, ferry and fishing vessel designs.
Depth of Discharge (DoD)ESS/EMS
The share of a battery's capacity that has been withdrawn in a discharge, expressed as a percentage; a battery discharged from 90 to 30 percent state of charge has seen a 60 percent DoD. Shallower cycling markedly extends cycle life, so marine systems are often operated within a restricted SoC window rather than from full to empty. DoD limits are set in the EMS strategy and reflected in the sizing margin of the installed battery.

E

Energy Density (Wh/kg)ESS/EMS
The amount of energy a battery stores per unit of mass (Wh/kg) or volume (Wh/l). At system level, marine batteries deliver considerably less than the bare cell figure because modules, racks, cooling, fire protection and converters add weight and volume. Energy density drives how much zero-emission range a ferry can carry within its deadweight and stability limits, and it is one of the main trade-offs between cell chemistries.
Energy Management System (EMS)ESS/EMS
The supervisory control layer that decides how a hybrid vessel's energy sources are used moment to moment — when to run engines, when to charge or discharge the battery, and how to split load for lowest fuel consumption or emissions. The EMS sits above the power management system and battery management system, optimising the plant against the vessel's operating mode.
Energy Storage System (ESS)ESS/EMS
The complete battery installation on board — cells, modules, racks, cooling, fire protection and the battery management system — engineered and class-approved as a single system. Marine ESS applications include spinning reserve, peak shaving, zero-emission harbour transits and blackout prevention, with class rules (e.g. DNV Battery notation) governing safety design and the crew training required to operate them.
Energy vs Power Battery DesignESS/EMS
The distinction between batteries optimised to store many kilowatt-hours (energy design) and batteries optimised to deliver high power relative to their capacity (power design). A battery ferry crossing a fjord needs an energy-optimised system for range, while a DP vessel using the battery for spinning reserve and peak shaving needs high C-rate power capability from a comparatively small pack. Cell selection, cooling and converter rating all follow from which duty dominates.
Enhanced Dynamic Performance (DP)ESS/EMS
The improvement in a DP vessel's power system response achieved by adding fast energy storage to the plant. The battery absorbs and supplies the rapid thruster load fluctuations caused by waves and DP control action, so the diesel engines see a smoother load and the system rides through sudden steps without frequency dips. This allows fewer generators online for the same safety level and is recognised in hybrid class notations and DP capability documentation.

F

Fuel Cell–Battery Hybrid SystemESS/EMS
A power plant that pairs fuel cells, typically hydrogen-fuelled, with a battery so each source works where it is strongest. The fuel cell supplies steady base load at its efficient operating point, while the battery handles transients, peak loads and start-up, protecting the fuel cell stack from load steps that shorten its life. This architecture is central to emerging zero-emission ferries and short-sea vessels, with the EMS deciding the power split between the two sources.

H

Hybrid Control StrategyESS/EMS
The set of rules or optimisation logic the EMS uses to decide, at every moment, how load is shared between engines, batteries and other sources. Typical strategies include charge-sustaining operation around a target state of charge, load-dependent start and stop of generators, peak shaving thresholds and dedicated modes for DP, transit, harbour and zero-emission operation. A well-tuned strategy is what converts installed hybrid hardware into actual fuel, maintenance and emissions savings.

I

Islanded Operation (Shipboard Microgrid)ESS/EMS
Operation of an electrical system with no connection to an external grid, which is the normal condition of a ship at sea. A vessel's power plant is effectively a small islanded microgrid: it must maintain its own voltage and frequency, balance generation against load in real time, and survive faults without outside support. Batteries strengthen an islanded system by providing instant reserve and, in DC-grid vessels, by helping form and stabilise the grid itself.

L

LFP vs NMC Cell ChemistryESS/EMS
The two lithium-ion chemistries most common in marine service. NMC (nickel manganese cobalt) cells offer higher energy density, giving more kilowatt-hours per tonne, and have dominated marine installations to date. LFP (lithium iron phosphate) cells are heavier per kilowatt-hour but are generally more thermally stable, tolerate cycling well and avoid cobalt, and are increasingly considered for newbuilds. The choice affects weight, battery space size, fire safety case and lifetime cost.
Lithium-Ion Battery (Marine)ESS/EMS
A rechargeable battery based on lithium-ion cells, engineered and type-approved for shipboard installation. Compared with land or automotive systems, marine batteries add class-required layers of protection: marinised modules and racks, a certified BMS, dedicated battery spaces with fire suppression, off-gas detection and ventilation. Lithium-ion is the dominant technology behind hybrid OSVs, battery-electric ferries and DP spinning-reserve installations because of its high efficiency, energy density and fast response.
Load LevellingESS/EMS
An EMS function in which the battery continuously smooths the load seen by the running engines, absorbing energy when demand dips and supplying it when demand rises. Where peak shaving targets occasional peaks, load levelling addresses the constant fluctuation of loads such as thrusters, cranes and heave-compensated winches. The engines can then run near a steady, efficient set point, reducing fuel consumption, exhaust emissions and mechanical wear.

M

Megawatt Charging System (MCS)ESS/EMS
Shore charging infrastructure capable of transferring power in the megawatt range, needed when a battery ferry must recover a full crossing's energy during a berth stay of only a few minutes. Norwegian fjord ferries pioneered this scale of charging, using automated connection systems, robotic plugs or induction, often supported by a shore-side battery buffer where the local grid is weak. High charge rates must be coordinated with the battery's C-rate limits and thermal management.

O

Off-Gas DetectionESS/EMS
Monitoring of the battery space for the gases a lithium-ion cell vents in the early stage of failure, before or during thermal runaway. Detecting these gases gives the crew warning ahead of smoke or fire, allowing the affected string to be disconnected and ventilation and suppression measures to be activated. Class rules for battery notations require off-gas detection in battery spaces, with alarms integrated into the vessel's safety and automation systems.
Opportunity ChargingESS/EMS
Charging the battery in the short windows a vessel's schedule naturally provides, such as ferry berth stops, lock transits or cargo operations alongside, rather than in one long overnight session. It lets a modest battery support an energy-intensive timetable, but demands high charging power, reliable automated connection and careful thermal management. The EMS plans each charge so the vessel always departs with enough energy for the next leg plus reserves.

P

Peak ShavingESS/EMS
An energy management function where the battery supplies the top slice of short-duration load peaks, so the generators only need to cover the average demand. Typical shipboard peaks come from thruster bursts in DP, crane lifts or winch operations. Peak shaving allows fewer or smaller engines to run at an efficient, steady load, cutting fuel consumption, emissions and running hours; the battery recharges in the load troughs between peaks.

S

Second-Life BatteriesESS/EMS
Battery systems or cells redeployed to a less demanding duty after they no longer meet the requirements of their first application, typically once capacity has faded to around 70–80 percent. A marine propulsion battery may find second life in shore-side energy storage or harbour charging buffers, spreading its embodied carbon and cost over more service years. Reuse on board is constrained by class type-approval and safety documentation, so most maritime second-life value today is realised ashore.
Shaft Generator (PTO/PTI)ESS/EMS
An electrical machine coupled to the main propulsion shaft or gearbox that can operate in two directions. In power take-off (PTO) mode it generates electricity from the efficient main engine, allowing auxiliary gensets to be shut down at sea; in power take-in (PTI) mode it works as a motor, boosting or taking over propulsion with power from gensets or the battery. Combined with frequency converters and an ESS, PTO/PTI is a cornerstone of modern hybrid machinery arrangements.
Shore ChargingESS/EMS
Transferring electrical energy from the shore grid into a vessel's battery while it is alongside, as distinct from shore power that merely feeds the hotel load. Charging systems range from manual cable connections for overnight charging to automated high-power systems that replenish a ferry in minutes at each berth. Norway's battery-electric ferry network is built on shore charging, and charger power, grid capacity and connection time are sizing parameters as important as the battery itself.
Spinning ReserveESS/EMS
Standby power capacity available fast enough to cover the sudden loss of a running generator. Traditionally this meant keeping extra engines online at low load, particularly in DP operations, which burns fuel inefficiently and adds running hours. A battery certified for the duty can provide this reserve instead, responding in milliseconds, so the vessel can safely operate with fewer generators connected; class societies accept batteries as spinning reserve under the relevant battery and DP notations.
State of Charge (SoC)ESS/EMS
The energy currently stored in a battery expressed as a percentage of its present usable capacity, analogous to a fuel gauge. The BMS estimates SoC from cell voltages, current integration and temperature, and the EMS uses it to schedule charging, enforce operating windows and verify that reserves for DP or the next ferry crossing are in place. Because operating between roughly 20 and 90 percent extends cell life, marine systems rarely use the full 0–100 percent range in normal service.
State of Health (SoH)ESS/EMS
A measure of a battery's condition relative to new, usually expressed as remaining capacity as a percentage of the original rated capacity, sometimes supplemented by internal resistance. SoH is tracked by the BMS and verified through periodic capacity testing, and it underpins class surveys, warranty claims and replacement planning. When SoH falls below the level needed for the vessel's duty, such as a full crossing or the certified spinning reserve, the system must be derated, augmented or replaced.
Supercapacitor (Ultracapacitor)ESS/EMS
An energy storage device that stores charge electrostatically rather than chemically, giving very high power density, near-instant response and cycle life in the hundreds of thousands, but only a small amount of energy. In marine systems supercapacitors suit duties with rapid, repetitive power swings, such as crane and winch load smoothing or ride-through of momentary disturbances. They are sometimes combined with batteries so the supercapacitor absorbs the sharpest transients while the battery covers the energy.

T

Thermal RunawayESS/EMS
A self-accelerating failure of a lithium-ion cell in which internal heating triggers chemical reactions that release more heat and flammable gases, potentially leading to fire and propagation to neighbouring cells. Triggers include internal defects, overcharge, external heating and mechanical damage. Marine battery design defends against it in layers: cell quality and BMS limits to prevent initiation, module design to resist propagation, and the battery space's off-gas detection, ventilation and fixed fire suppression to contain the consequences.

U

Usable Energy (Net Capacity)ESS/EMS
The portion of a battery's installed energy that is actually available for operations after subtracting the reserved margins at the top and bottom of the state-of-charge window, converter losses and any capacity held back for safety functions such as spinning reserve. A ferry with 2 MWh installed may plan its crossings on well under that figure. Distinguishing installed from usable energy is essential in tender specifications, route energy budgets and end-of-life assessments.

V

Variable-Speed GenerationESS/EMS
Running a generator set at whatever engine speed best matches the current load, instead of the fixed speed required to hold 50 or 60 Hz on a conventional AC switchboard. This is enabled by connecting the generator through power converters, most naturally on a DC-grid vessel, and it cuts fuel consumption markedly at the part loads where OSVs and ferries spend much of their time. Combined with an ESS, it lets each engine operate near its optimum while the battery handles transients.

Z

Zero-Emission Operation (Battery Mode)ESS/EMS
An operating mode in which all propulsion and hotel load is supplied from the battery with every combustion engine stopped, producing no exhaust emissions and little noise. It is used for fjord crossings, harbour transits, port stays and environmentally restricted areas such as the Norwegian World Heritage fjords, where zero-emission requirements are being phased in. The EMS supervises the mode, ensuring enough energy remains to complete the planned operation and to restart engines or reach a charging point with margin.
