3

3-2-1 Backup RuleCYBER
A widely used guideline for resilient backups: keep three copies of your data, on two different types of media, with one copy stored off-site. For a vessel this might mean the live system, a local backup server, and a copy replicated ashore when bandwidth allows. Following the rule ensures that a single event — ransomware, hardware failure, fire or flooding — cannot destroy every copy at once.

4

4G/LTE Coastal ConnectivityCYBER
Cellular data links that ships use when operating near shore, often switching over automatically from satellite to save cost and gain bandwidth. Each cellular modem is an additional internet gateway that may bypass the ship's main firewall if not routed through the same security stack as the VSAT link. Vessels should treat 4G/LTE routers as untrusted perimeter devices: inventory them, disable default credentials, and ensure traffic passes through the ship's central firewall regardless of which bearer is active.

A

AAA — Authentication, Authorisation, AccountingCYBER
A framework combining three access-control functions: verifying who a user is (authentication), determining what they may do (authorisation), and recording what they actually did (accounting). Together these answer the questions "who logged in, what did they touch, and when" — essential for investigating incidents on ship and shore networks. On vessels, AAA is often weakened by shared bridge or engine-room accounts, which makes the accounting element meaningless.
Acceptable Use PolicyCYBER
A document that tells crew and shore staff what they may and may not do with company IT equipment, networks and internet connections. On board, it typically covers use of the vessel's business network, personal devices on crew Wi-Fi, USB sticks, social media and streaming over satellite links. A good acceptable use policy is short, written in plain language, and signed as part of onboarding so every seafarer knows the rules before they connect anything.
Access ControlCYBER
The practice of restricting who and what can use shipboard systems, networks and data, typically through user accounts, passwords, key cards and role-based permissions. On board, this means separating the master's administrative rights from crew accounts, limiting who can log on to bridge and engine-room workstations, and controlling vendor access to control systems. Weak access control, such as shared passwords on ECDIS or automation stations, remains one of the most common findings in maritime cyber audits.
Account Lockout PolicyCYBER
A rule that temporarily disables a user account after a set number of failed login attempts, blocking password-guessing and brute-force attacks. Lockout policies need careful tuning in maritime settings: locking the shared ECDIS or DP operator account at a critical moment could create a safety problem worse than the one being prevented. Many fleets therefore combine short lockout timers with alerting rather than indefinite lockouts on operational systems.
Advanced Persistent Threat (APT)CYBER
A well-resourced attacker, often state-backed, that gains long-term covert access to a network and pursues a specific objective over months or years. In the maritime sector, APTs typically target shipping companies, ports and shipbuilders for cargo data, vessel movements, sanctioned-trade intelligence or naval technology. Their hallmark is patience: quiet reconnaissance and persistence rather than immediate, visible damage.
Adversarial Machine LearningCYBER
Techniques for deceiving or degrading machine-learning systems, for example by feeding them subtly manipulated inputs that cause misclassification. For autonomous and remotely operated vessels this is a direct safety concern: a perception system trained to recognise buoys, small craft or navigation marks could be fooled by crafted visual patterns or poisoned training data. Testing perception and decision systems against adversarial inputs is becoming part of assurance work for autonomous shipping.
AI in Cyber DefenceCYBER
The use of machine learning to support security operations, typically by detecting unusual behaviour in network traffic, user activity or system logs faster than human analysts could. In a fleet context, AI-assisted monitoring can flag anomalies across many vessels with limited bandwidth and no onboard security staff. It is a support tool rather than a replacement for skilled analysts: models generate false positives and still require people who understand both the technology and shipboard operations to judge what matters.
AI-Enabled AttacksCYBER
Cyber attacks that use artificial intelligence to increase their scale, speed or credibility — for example convincingly written phishing emails in any language, voice cloning of a company executive to authorise a payment, or automated discovery of vulnerable systems. For shipping companies the practical effect is that crude, easily spotted fraud attempts are being replaced by polished ones referencing real vessels, ports and counterparties. Defences shift towards verification procedures that do not depend on how genuine a message looks or sounds.
Air GapCYBER
A security measure in which a critical system, such as a vessel's automation or cargo control network, has no physical or wireless connection to other networks or the internet. An air gap greatly reduces remote attack risk, but it is rarely absolute on modern ships: USB sticks used for chart updates, vendor laptops and retrofitted remote-monitoring links routinely bridge supposedly isolated systems. Crews should treat an air gap as one layer of protection to be verified regularly, not as a guarantee of safety.
AIS SpoofingCYBER
The transmission of false Automatic Identification System messages to create ghost vessels, hide a ship's true identity or falsify its reported position. Because AIS was designed without authentication, spoofed targets can appear on ECDIS and radar overlays and mislead watchkeepers, VTS operators and fleet tracking services. Bridge teams should cross-check AIS data against radar, visual observation and other independent sources before acting on it.
Alarm Management (Security Aspects)CYBER
The discipline of ensuring that alarms from engine room, bridge, and automation systems remain trustworthy and manageable, including under cyber attack. An attacker who can suppress, delay, or flood alarms can hide a developing machinery failure or overwhelm the watchkeeper with nuisance alarms until real ones are ignored. Security-aware alarm management means protecting alarm servers and networks from tampering, logging alarm inhibits and acknowledgements, and training crew to treat unexplained alarm floods or silences as possible cyber events, not just software glitches.
Alert FatigueCYBER
The gradual desensitisation that sets in when people receive too many warnings, most of which turn out to be false alarms. Watchkeepers already know this from bridge and engine room alarm systems, and the same effect applies to security alerts: if an antivirus or monitoring tool cries wolf constantly, the one alert that matters gets dismissed too. Reducing alert fatigue means tuning systems so alarms are few, meaningful and actionable.
Anti-Malware / AntivirusCYBER
Software that detects, blocks and removes malicious programs such as viruses, worms, trojans and ransomware, using known signatures and behavioural analysis. On ships, antivirus is a first line of defence on crew computers, planning stations and any system exposed to USB sticks or email. Its main limitation at sea is that signature updates depend on connectivity, so vessels with infrequent updates should not rely on antivirus alone.
API Security (Ship-Shore)CYBER
Protecting the application programming interfaces (APIs) through which shipboard systems, fleet platforms, ports and third-party services exchange data — voyage plans, performance data, noon reports, cargo information. Weakly secured APIs can expose fleet data at scale or provide a path from a shore platform toward vessel systems. Good practice includes strong authentication for every API call, limiting each integration to the data it actually needs, and logging access so misuse can be detected.
Application WhitelistingCYBER
A control that allows only pre-approved software to run on a computer, blocking everything else by default. It is well suited to shipboard OT and navigation workstations, which run a fixed set of applications and change rarely, unlike office PCs. On an ECDIS or automation station, whitelisting can stop malware introduced via USB from executing even when antivirus signatures are out of date.
Asset Management (Cyber)CYBER
The practice of maintaining an accurate, up-to-date inventory of all hardware, software and network connections across the fleet — from office servers to the ECDIS, engine control systems and every switch in between. You cannot protect what you do not know you have, so the asset register is the foundation for patching, risk assessment and incident response. For ships this includes recording which systems are onboard each vessel, their software versions, and who is responsible for maintaining them.
Attack Path MappingCYBER
The practice of charting the realistic routes an attacker could take from an initial foothold — a phishing email, a compromised supplier, an exposed remote-access service — to a valuable target such as a vessel's automation network or a company's finance system. For ships, typical paths run through business networks, remote-maintenance connections and shared USB media into OT systems. Mapping these paths shows which defences actually break the chain, helping prioritise limited security budgets.
Attack SurfaceCYBER
The sum of all points where an attacker could try to enter or extract data from a vessel's systems, including satellite links, crew Wi-Fi, USB ports, vendor remote-access connections and shore-side fleet management platforms. Every new connected device, from smart sensors to remote engine monitoring, enlarges the attack surface of the ship. Mapping and reducing this surface, for example by disabling unused ports and services, is a first step in any shipboard cyber risk assessment.
AuthenticationCYBER
The process of verifying that a user, device or system is who or what it claims to be, typically with passwords, cryptographic keys, certificates or biometrics. It is the gatekeeper for everything else in security: authorisation decisions are only meaningful if the identity behind them is genuine. Shipboard practice has historically been weak here — shared logins on bridge and ECDIS workstations are common — and tightening authentication is one of the most cost-effective improvements a shipping company can make.
AuthorisationCYBER
The process of deciding what an authenticated user or system is allowed to do — which files to read, which settings to change, which systems to reach. It follows the principle of least privilege: people and software should have only the access their role requires. On a vessel this means, for example, that a crew welfare laptop should never be able to reach the automation network, and a service technician's remote account should open only the specific system under maintenance.
Autonomous Vessel CybersecurityCYBER
The security discipline for vessels operated with reduced crew, remote control or full autonomy, where a cyber compromise can translate directly into loss of navigational control. Because there may be no crew aboard to intervene, the integrity of sensors, decision software and the ship-shore communication link becomes safety-critical, and requirements for redundancy, secure fallback states and link protection are correspondingly stricter. Classification societies and flag states increasingly treat cybersecurity as a core element of approving autonomous operations, alongside traditional seaworthiness.

B

BackdoorCYBER
A hidden way into a system that bypasses normal authentication, either planted by an attacker or left behind as an undocumented vendor access path. On vessels, forgotten remote-maintenance accounts in ECDIS, automation or DP systems can act as de facto backdoors. Once in place, a backdoor lets an attacker return at will, even after the original vulnerability is patched.
Backup and RecoveryCYBER
The process of keeping secure copies of critical data and system configurations so they can be restored after a cyber incident, hardware failure or ransomware attack. For ships this covers ECDIS charts and routes, loading computer data, PMS records and automation system configurations, with at least one copy kept offline or ashore. Recovery procedures should be tested periodically, since a backup that has never been restored under drill conditions may fail when it is needed at sea.
Ballast Water Treatment System SecurityCYBER
Protection of the PLC-controlled treatment plants (UV, electrochlorination, or filtration) that ships must run to meet the Ballast Water Management Convention. These systems are often installed as retrofit packages with their own controllers, remote-support modems, and USB service ports, and are easily overlooked in the ship's cyber inventory. Compromise or malfunction can interfere with ballast operations, affect stability planning, and create port state control problems if treatment records are corrupted.
Bastion Host / Jump ServerCYBER
A single, hardened computer that acts as the controlled entry point into a protected network — all remote sessions must pass through it before reaching anything inside. In fleet operations, a jump server is the standard way to let vendors or shore engineers reach onboard automation or DP systems: they log in to the bastion, are authenticated and recorded, and only then connect onward. Because it concentrates all access in one place, it is heavily monitored and stripped of unnecessary software.
Big Data Security (Fleet Performance Data)CYBER
Protecting the large volumes of operational data modern fleets generate — fuel consumption, engine telemetry, positions, weather routing, cargo records — as it is collected on board, transmitted ashore and analysed in cloud platforms. This data has commercial value: aggregated voyage and performance data can reveal charter strategies, cargo flows and technical condition to competitors or attackers. Security concerns include who owns the data, which third parties (engine makers, analytics vendors, charterers) can access it, and whether its integrity can be trusted for decisions such as performance claims or emissions reporting.
BIMCO Guidelines on Cyber Security Onboard ShipsCYBER
An industry guidance document produced by BIMCO together with other shipping associations, offering practical advice on identifying, protecting against and responding to cyber threats on board. The guidelines translate high-level frameworks into shipboard terms, covering topics such as network layout, remote access, crew awareness and incident response. Many companies use them as the working reference when implementing IMO cyber risk requirements in their safety management systems.
Biometric AuthenticationCYBER
Verifying identity using a physical characteristic — fingerprint, face or iris — instead of, or in addition to, a password. Biometrics are convenient because they cannot be forgotten or easily shared, which suits shipboard environments where crew rotate frequently and shared passwords are a chronic weakness. They work best as one factor in multi-factor authentication rather than as the sole means of access.
Blockchain in Shipping (Security Aspects)CYBER
The use of distributed-ledger technology in shipping — most visibly for electronic bills of lading and cargo documentation — viewed through a security lens. A blockchain makes recorded entries very hard to alter afterwards, which suits documents of title, but it does not guarantee that data was correct when entered, and the surrounding platforms, user accounts and private keys remain conventional attack targets. In practice the security of a blockchain-based shipping service depends far more on the operator's platform security and key management than on the ledger itself.
Blue TeamCYBER
The defenders: the people responsible for monitoring systems, detecting intrusions and responding to attacks, whether real or simulated. In a shipping company the blue team role may sit with the IT department, a managed security provider, or a fleet operations centre watching vessel networks. During security exercises the blue team practises spotting and containing the red team's simulated attacks.
BotnetCYBER
A network of compromised computers or devices controlled remotely by an attacker, often used to send spam, mine cryptocurrency or launch denial-of-service attacks. Shipboard PCs and poorly secured connected devices can be silently recruited into botnets, consuming scarce satellite bandwidth and exposing the vessel's network to further compromise. Unusual outbound traffic over the VSAT link is a common first sign that an onboard machine has been enrolled in a botnet.
Brute-Force AttackCYBER
An attack that tries many password or key combinations, often automatically, until one works. Internet-exposed services common in shipping, such as remote-access portals, VSAT management interfaces and crew webmail, are routine brute-force targets. Strong passwords, account lockouts and multi-factor authentication make this approach largely impractical.
Bug Bounty & Responsible DisclosureCYBER
Structured ways for outside researchers to report security flaws they find in a company's systems or products. A responsible disclosure policy gives researchers a safe, legal channel to report a vulnerability so it can be fixed before it is exploited; a bug bounty adds a financial reward. For maritime suppliers of navigation, automation or fleet software, having a disclosure route matters — without one, a researcher who finds a flaw in a widely installed shipboard system has no good way to warn anyone.
Business Continuity Plan (BCP)CYBER
A documented plan for keeping essential operations running when a major disruption hits — including a cyber attack that takes down email, planning systems or vessel communications. For a shipping company this means knowing how to charter, crew, bunker and communicate with vessels using fallback methods such as phone, paper and alternative channels. The plan should be tested regularly, because a BCP that only exists on the same server the attackers just encrypted is no plan at all.
Business Email Compromise (BEC)CYBER
Fraud in which attackers use a compromised or convincingly spoofed business email account to trick staff into making payments or changing bank details. Shipping is a prime target because large payments to unfamiliar counterparties are normal: bunker invoices, charter hire, port disbursements and agency fees. A typical maritime BEC asks the accounts team to pay a legitimate-looking invoice to a "updated" bank account controlled by the criminals. Verifying payment changes through a second, independent channel is the standard defence.
Business Impact AnalysisCYBER
A structured exercise that works out which business processes and systems matter most, and what it would cost — in money, safety and reputation — if each one were unavailable for hours, days or weeks. The results drive sensible priorities: it tells you whether the crewing system, the planned maintenance system or the charter party archive needs faster recovery. For vessel operations it also identifies which onboard systems a ship can safely sail without, and for how long.
BYOD — Crew Personal DevicesCYBER
The reality that every crew member brings phones, tablets, laptops, and USB gadgets on board, and that these devices will connect to whatever network or charging port is available. Personal devices are outside the company's control, may already be infected, and are a common route for malware to reach business or even OT networks. Sound practice is to confine personal devices to an isolated crew network, ban them from OT and navigation equipment ports, and make the rules explicit in the ship's cyber security procedures rather than relying on common sense.

C

CAN BusCYBER
A robust serial bus originally developed for vehicles and now common in marine engines, thrusters, small-craft networks, and as the physical layer beneath NMEA 2000. CAN has no built-in authentication: any node on the bus can transmit messages that other nodes will accept as genuine, so a single compromised or rogue device can inject false engine commands or sensor values. Because retrofitting security onto CAN is impractical, protection depends on controlling physical access to the bus and vetting every device connected to it.
Cargo Control System SecurityCYBER
Protection of the systems that monitor and control cargo operations — tank gauging, valve and pump control, inert gas, and loading computers — on tankers, gas carriers, and bulk vessels. These systems increasingly exchange data with terminals and shore offices during loading, which creates a direct ship-shore attack path into safety-critical equipment. Manipulated tank readings or valve commands can cause overfills, stability problems, or pollution, so cargo control networks warrant strict segregation, controlled update procedures, and verified backups of the loading computer.
Cascading Effects (Port & Supply Chain)CYBER
The way a cyber incident at one node in the logistics chain spreads disruption far beyond its origin. When a terminal operating system or port community system goes down, vessels queue at anchor, trucks stack at gates, warehouses overflow and cargo owners further inland feel the delay within days — as incidents at major container terminals and carriers have repeatedly shown. Recognising these effects drives regulation such as the EU's NIS2 Directive, which treats ports and shipping as critical entities precisely because their failures cascade.
Certificate Expiry ManagementCYBER
The practice of tracking when digital certificates expire and renewing them before they lapse. An expired certificate can silently break encrypted links between ship and shore systems, block software updates, or trigger security warnings that crew learn to ignore. Fleets with many vessels and vendors typically keep a certificate inventory with automated renewal reminders, since a forgotten certificate on one ship can disable a service across the whole fleet.
Chain of CustodyCYBER
The documented record of who collected, handled and stored a piece of evidence, from the moment it was seized until it is presented to investigators, insurers or a court. After a cyber incident on board, this means logging when a laptop, USB stick or system image was collected, by whom, and how it was kept secure in transit ashore. A broken chain of custody can render evidence useless in legal or insurance proceedings, so masters and superintendents should follow the company's evidence-handling procedure rather than improvising.
Change Management (Cyber)CYBER
A controlled process for making changes to IT systems — software updates, new equipment, configuration changes — so that each change is assessed, approved, tested and documented before it goes live. Uncontrolled changes are a common cause of both outages and security holes, for example a firewall rule added "temporarily" during a port call and never removed. For fleet IT, change management also ensures shore and vessel systems stay consistent so support staff know exactly what is running where.
Chart & Software Update Path SecurityCYBER
Control over how ENC chart updates, weather files, and equipment software reach the ship's navigation and OT systems — whether by USB stick, DVD, email attachment, or online update service. The update path is a classic infection route: a corrupted chart medium prepared ashore can carry malware straight to the ECDIS. Good practice includes obtaining updates only from official distributors, scanning removable media on a dedicated quarantine station, verifying checksums or digital signatures where available, and logging every update applied to bridge equipment.
CIS Critical Security ControlsCYBER
A prioritised set of defensive actions published by the Center for Internet Security, widely used as a practical checklist for hardening IT and OT environments. Shipping companies often map the controls — such as asset inventory, secure configuration and access management — onto shore offices and vessel networks as a starting point before adopting heavier frameworks. Because the controls are ranked by impact, they help fleets with limited cyber budgets decide what to fix first.
CISO — Chief Information Security OfficerCYBER
The senior executive accountable for an organisation's information and cyber security strategy, budget and risk reporting. In larger shipping companies this is a dedicated role; in smaller ones the responsibility often sits with the IT manager, DPA or technical director wearing an additional hat. What matters is that someone with management authority owns cyber risk across both the office and the fleet, and reports on it to top management — not that the title exists on paper.
Class Cyber NotationsCYBER
Voluntary class notations that certify a vessel's cyber risk management against a classification society's rules, beyond the mandatory baseline. Examples include DNV's Cyber Secure notation (with levels such as Essential and Advanced), ABS CyberSafety and Lloyd's Register's cyber-related descriptive notes; all major class societies now offer comparable schemes. A notation is awarded after document review and survey, and is increasingly requested by charterers and insurers as evidence of cyber maturity.
Cloud Security (Maritime)CYBER
Securing the cloud services shipping companies increasingly rely on for fleet management, performance monitoring, planned maintenance, crewing and document handling. Responsibility is shared: the cloud provider secures the underlying infrastructure, while the shipping company remains responsible for configuring access controls, protecting accounts and deciding what data goes where. Misconfigured storage and weakly protected admin accounts — not flaws in the cloud platforms themselves — cause most maritime cloud incidents, and data-residency rules may constrain where fleet data can be stored.
Command and Control (C2)CYBER
The communication channel malware uses to receive instructions from, and send stolen data back to, the attacker's infrastructure. C2 traffic is often disguised as ordinary web traffic to evade detection. On ships, C2 connections must ride the satellite link, which is one reason monitoring shore-to-ship traffic for unusual destinations and patterns is a key detection opportunity for fleet operators.
Communication Plan (Incident)CYBER
A pre-agreed plan for who says what, to whom, and when during a cyber incident. It covers internal lines (vessel to DPA, IT to management), and external ones: flag state, class, insurers, charterers, port authorities, media and possibly national authorities under reporting regulations. It should include out-of-band contact methods — printed phone lists, alternative email — because the normal channels may be exactly what the attackers control. Deciding these things in advance prevents confusion and contradictory statements under pressure.
Compensating CountermeasureCYBER
An alternative security control applied when a required control cannot be implemented as specified — a common situation on ships, where legacy bridge or automation systems cannot be patched or replaced mid-service. Instead of the missing control, the risk is reduced by other means such as network segregation, removable-media policies or physical access restrictions. IEC 62443 and class rules formally recognise compensating countermeasures, provided the residual risk is assessed and documented.
Condition-Based Monitoring LinksCYBER
Data connections that stream vibration, temperature, performance, and fuel data from shipboard machinery to shore-based analytics platforms run by owners, OEMs, or class societies. The monitoring itself only needs data to flow off the ship, so the link should be engineered as one-way from the OT network outward, ideally through a DMZ or data diode. Problems arise when monitoring gateways are installed with bidirectional remote access enabled by default, quietly turning a sensor feed into an unmanaged entry point to the machinery network.
Configuration Baseline / Gold ImageCYBER
A documented, approved reference state for a system — its settings, software versions and security hardening — often captured as a ready-to-deploy "gold image". When a bridge PC or automation workstation needs rebuilding, restoring from the gold image guarantees a known-good, secure starting point instead of an improvised setup. Comparing running systems against the baseline also reveals unauthorised changes, which is valuable evidence during incident investigations and class surveys.
Credential StuffingCYBER
An attack that takes username-password pairs leaked from one breach and automatically tries them against other services, exploiting the habit of password reuse. A crew member or shore employee who reuses a personal password on the company VPN or email can hand attackers a working login without any hacking of the company itself. Multi-factor authentication and unique passwords per service defeat it.
Crew Cyber CompetenceCYBER TRAINING
The practical knowledge and skills seafarers need to operate connected shipboard systems safely: recognising phishing, handling USB media correctly, knowing what to do when a navigation or automation system behaves strangely, and knowing whom to report to. It goes beyond a one-off awareness course — competence should be built into familiarisation, drills and appraisals like any other shipboard skill. Companies increasingly define cyber competence requirements per rank, since a master, an ETO and a cook face different risks and responsibilities.
Crew Privacy & Data ProtectionCYBER REG
The obligation to handle seafarers' personal data — medical records, payroll, certificates, travel documents, welfare communications — lawfully and securely. For companies in or trading with Europe, GDPR applies to crew data just as it does ashore, including data processed by manning agents and third-party crewing systems. Monitoring of crew internet use or communications must also be proportionate and transparent. Getting this wrong damages trust on board and can trigger regulatory penalties.
Crew Wi-Fi SegregationCYBER
Keeping the wireless network provided for crew welfare completely separate from the ship's business and OT networks. Crew Wi-Fi carries unmanaged personal devices, streaming traffic, and whatever malware those devices bring aboard, so it must be treated as a hostile network even though it lives inside the hull. Segregation means separate VLANs or physical infrastructure, its own internet breakout with content filtering, and no route whatsoever from the crew network to automation, navigation, or administrative systems.
Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)CYBER
A web application flaw that lets an attacker inject malicious script into pages viewed by other users, typically to steal session cookies or credentials. For a shipping company this matters in customer-facing portals such as booking, tracking and crewing systems, where a successful XSS attack can hijack legitimate user sessions. It is fixed in the application code by properly validating and encoding user-supplied input.
Cryptographic Key ManagementCYBER
The generation, distribution, storage, rotation and revocation of the cryptographic keys that all encryption depends on. Encryption is only as strong as the handling of its keys: a stolen or never-changed key quietly defeats otherwise sound protection. In maritime settings this covers everything from satellite-link encryption keys and VPN certificates to the signing keys behind electronic certificates and e-navigation data, and it becomes harder when equipment is spread across a fleet that rarely visits a home port.
CryptojackingCYBER
The unauthorised use of someone else's computers to mine cryptocurrency for the attacker's profit. It rarely destroys data but steals processing power and electricity, degrades performance and signals that the network has been penetrated. On a vessel it can also consume scarce satellite bandwidth; a cryptojacking find should always prompt a wider investigation, since the same access could deliver worse payloads.
CSIRT / CERTCYBER
A Computer Security Incident Response Team (or Computer Emergency Response Team): a designated group that handles cyber incidents — analysing what happened, containing the damage and coordinating recovery. National CERTs, such as NorCERT in Norway, also issue warnings and assist companies in their country or sector. A shipping company rarely needs its own full-time CSIRT, but it should know in advance which internal people and external providers form its response team, and how to reach its national CERT when an incident exceeds in-house capability.
CSO / SSO / CySO Role DistinctionCYBER
Three related security roles that should not be confused. The Company Security Officer (CSO) and Ship Security Officer (SSO) are ISPS Code roles focused on physical ship security, ashore and on board respectively, while the Cybersecurity Officer (CySO) is the person accountable for cybersecurity — a designation formally required for US-flagged vessels under the USCG cybersecurity rule. In practice the roles may be combined, but responsibilities, training and reporting lines for each must be clearly defined in company documentation.
CVE and CVSSCYBER
CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) is the public catalogue that assigns a unique identifier to each known software vulnerability, while CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) rates its severity on a scale of 0 to 10. Fleet IT teams and equipment vendors use CVE numbers to track which weaknesses affect onboard software, from operating systems to automation packages, and CVSS scores to prioritise patching. A high CVSS score on a system reachable from the ship's business network deserves prompt attention even if the equipment itself seems obscure.
Cyber Drill RequirementsCYBER
Obligations to periodically exercise the crew's and company's response to a cyber incident, in the same spirit as fire or abandon-ship drills. The USCG cybersecurity rule requires drills and exercises for US-flagged vessels and regulated facilities, and industry guidance recommends scenario-based drills such as loss of ECDIS, GPS spoofing or ransomware in the planned maintenance system. Drills should be logged, evaluated and used to update the cybersecurity plan and incident response procedures.
Cyber HygieneCYBER
The routine, everyday practices that keep shipboard systems secure: using strong individual passwords, locking workstations, scanning removable media, applying updates and reporting anything unusual. Like good seamanship, cyber hygiene is less about advanced technology than about consistent habits across the whole crew. Most reported maritime cyber incidents begin with a lapse in basic hygiene rather than a sophisticated attack.
Cyber Incident ReportingCYBER
The formal obligation to notify authorities when a cyber incident affects a ship or port facility, separate from any internal reporting. Under the USCG rule, US-flagged vessels and MTSA-regulated facilities must report reportable cyber incidents to the National Response Center, while EU operators may have parallel duties to national authorities under the NIS2 regime. Companies should define in advance what counts as reportable, who reports, and within what deadline, since obligations can differ by flag, port state and charter party.
Cyber Incident Response PlanCYBER
A documented procedure describing how ship and shore staff detect, contain, report and recover from a cyber incident. IACS UR E26 and the USCG cybersecurity rule both expect incident response and recovery capabilities to be planned in advance, including fallback to manual operation of critical systems such as steering, propulsion and navigation. A good shipboard plan assigns roles for the master, officers and shore support, and is tested through drills rather than left on the shelf.
Cyber Insurance (Marine)CYBER
Insurance cover for losses arising from cyber incidents, such as business interruption, incident response costs, data restoration and certain liabilities. In the marine market this is complicated: traditional hull and P&I policies have historically excluded or limited cyber-related losses, so shipowners may need standalone cyber cover or specific buy-back clauses. Insurers increasingly ask detailed questions about a company's security controls before quoting, so good cyber hygiene directly affects both premium and insurability.
Cyber Kill ChainCYBER
A model describing the typical stages of an attack, from reconnaissance and weaponisation through delivery, exploitation, installation, command and control, and finally action on the objective. Its value for a shipping company is that breaking any single link, for example blocking the phishing email at delivery or the C2 callback over the satellite link, can stop the whole attack. It underpins layered "defence in depth" thinking in maritime cyber risk management.
Cyber Notation SurveyCYBER
The class survey performed to grant or maintain a voluntary cyber notation on a vessel. Surveyors verify that the documented cyber security arrangements — network drawings, asset inventories, access control, incident procedures — match what is actually installed and practised on board, typically at initial assignment and then at periodic renewals. Findings are handled like other class survey deficiencies and must be closed for the notation to remain valid.
Cyber ResilienceCYBER
The ability of a ship, company or port to continue delivering its essential functions during a cyber incident and to recover quickly afterwards — accepting that some attacks will succeed despite good defences. For a vessel this means being able to navigate, communicate and manage cargo safely even if key digital systems are compromised, using backups, manual procedures and degraded modes. Resilience is built through preparation: tested recovery plans, offline backups, crews drilled in operating without their usual systems, and honest exercises rather than paperwork.
Cyber Risk Management (IMO Resolution MSC.428(98))CYBER
The IMO requirement that cyber risks be addressed within the ship's Safety Management System, verified through ISM audits since 2021. In practice this means identifying critical IT and OT systems, assessing threats and vulnerabilities, implementing protective and detective controls, and maintaining response and recovery plans — supported by industry guidance such as the BIMCO/ICS Guidelines on Cyber Security Onboard Ships.
Cyber Risk RegisterCYBER
A living document listing the company's identified cyber risks, each with an assessment of likelihood and consequence, the controls in place, the risk owner, and any actions planned. It should cover both office IT and vessel systems — for example "ransomware in the planned maintenance system" or "unauthorised remote access to the engine automation network". Shipping companies familiar with ISM-style risk assessment will recognise the format; the point is to treat cyber risks with the same discipline as navigational or mechanical ones.
Cyber Security Management System (CSMS)CYBER
The structured set of policies, procedures, responsibilities and records through which a shipping company manages cyber risk across its fleet, analogous to the safety management system under the ISM Code. A CSMS typically covers governance, risk assessment, technical controls, training, supplier management and incident response, and may be built on frameworks such as ISO/IEC 27001 or IEC 62443. Auditors, class societies and vetting inspectors increasingly ask to see the CSMS rather than isolated technical measures.
Cyber Situational Awareness (Fleet)CYBER
A current, accurate picture of the cyber state of an entire fleet: which systems are running where, what their vulnerabilities are, what unusual activity is occurring, and which external threats are relevant right now. It combines technical monitoring from vessels with threat intelligence and vulnerability data, ideally presented so that both security teams and fleet managers can act on it. Limited satellite bandwidth and the variety of systems across a fleet make this genuinely hard, which is why many operators start with their most critical vessels and systems.
Cyber Threat Landscape ReportsCYBER
Periodic publications that summarise current attack trends, threat actors and notable incidents for a sector, such as ENISA's transport threat landscape or the annual maritime cyber threat reports from industry and academic groups. For shipping companies they provide an evidence base for risk assessments and board reporting: which attack types are actually hitting the industry, rather than what is merely theoretically possible. Read critically, they help direct spending toward the threats — commonly ransomware, phishing and fraud — that dominate real maritime incident data.
Cyber Type ApprovalCYBER
Certification by a classification society that a product type — such as an automation controller, navigation console or network device — meets defined cyber security requirements, so it can be installed on ships seeking cyber notations or complying with IACS UR E27. The supplier submits design documentation and test evidence, and the approval covers the product line rather than an individual installation. Type-approved components simplify newbuild approval because the yard does not have to re-demonstrate each device's security features.
Cyber-Physical AttackCYBER
An attack that uses digital means to cause physical consequences — manipulating a ship's steering or propulsion controls, altering ballast operations, interfering with crane movements in a terminal, or corrupting sensor data so that operators make dangerous decisions. It is the point where cybersecurity stops being an IT problem and becomes a safety problem, since the potential outcomes include collision, grounding, pollution and injury. Defending against it centres on isolating and monitoring OT systems and ensuring safe manual fallbacks exist and are practised.
Cybersecurity AssessmentCYBER
A systematic evaluation of a vessel's or company's computer systems, networks and procedures to identify vulnerabilities and rank cyber risks. The USCG cybersecurity rule requires periodic cybersecurity assessments for US-flagged vessels and regulated facilities, and the results feed directly into the cybersecurity plan. On board, an assessment typically covers bridge, engine room and cargo systems, remote access paths, and crew practices such as USB use.
Cybersecurity AuditCYBER
An independent check that a company's cyber security arrangements exist, are followed and are effective, performed against a chosen reference such as ISO/IEC 27001, the company CSMS or flag requirements. Unlike a technical assessment, an audit focuses on evidence: records of training, patching, drills, access reviews and incident handling, both ashore and on sample vessels. Audit findings are tracked to closure and are often reviewed by flag administrations, charterers and vetting schemes.
Cybersecurity BudgetingCYBER
The process of allocating money for security across the fleet in a deliberate, risk-based way rather than as leftover IT spend. Maritime budgets have particular rhythms: network segregation and OT upgrades are often only practical at drydock, so they must be planned years ahead in the docking budget, while training, monitoring services and insurance are recurring operating costs. A useful test is whether spending maps to the risk register — money should go to the risks the company has actually identified, not just to whatever vendors are selling.
Cybersecurity Certification SchemesCYBER
Formal programmes under which an accredited body certifies that an organisation, ship or product meets a defined cyber security standard — for example ISO/IEC 27001 certification of a shipping company, IEC 62443 certification of an automation product, or a class society cyber notation. Certification provides third-party assurance that owners can show to charterers, insurers and authorities instead of each party performing its own audit. The scope and limits of each certificate matter: certifying the shore office does not by itself certify the fleet.
Cybersecurity in Newbuild ContractsCYBER
Contract clauses and technical specifications that make cyber security a deliverable of the shipyard and its suppliers, not an afterthought for the owner. For ships contracted from 1 July 2024, IACS UR E26 and E27 apply mandatorily, so newbuild specifications should reference them explicitly and define who delivers network drawings, software inventories, security test reports and type approvals. Owners increasingly also specify cyber notation targets, FAT/SAT cyber testing and handover of credentials and documentation at delivery.
Cybersecurity Maturity ModelCYBER
A framework for measuring how developed an organisation's security practices are, typically on a scale from ad hoc ("we react when something breaks") to optimised ("we measure, review and improve continuously"). Maturity models help a shipping company benchmark itself, set realistic improvement targets and show progress to the board, charterers and insurers. The goal is not to reach the top level everywhere, but to reach the level that matches the company's risk exposure — a DP construction vessel operator needs more maturity around OT than a small domestic ferry company.
Cybersecurity Officer (CySO)CYBER
The person designated as accountable for cybersecurity for a vessel or facility, a role formally required by the USCG rule "Cybersecurity in the Marine Transportation System" for US-flagged vessels and MTSA-regulated facilities. The CySO develops and maintains the cybersecurity plan, ensures training and drills are carried out, and acts as the point of contact for incident reporting. The role may be held ashore and cover several vessels, but it must be clearly assigned and the person must be reachable.
Cybersecurity Plan (Vessel)CYBER
A documented plan describing how cyber risks to a specific vessel are managed: the systems covered, protective measures, responsibilities, training, drills and incident response arrangements. The USCG cybersecurity rule requires US-flagged vessels to implement such plans on a phased timeline, informed by the cybersecurity assessment and maintained by the CySO. The plan sits alongside the Ship Security Plan and safety management documentation, and should reflect the actual equipment on board rather than a generic fleet template.
Cybersecurity Training RequirementsCYBER
Requirements that seafarers and shore staff receive training matched to their cyber responsibilities, from basic awareness (phishing, USB hygiene, password practice) to role-specific training for officers and the CySO. The USCG cybersecurity rule mandates cybersecurity training for personnel of US-flagged vessels and regulated facilities, and industry guidance such as the BIMCO/ICS guidelines recommends training for all crew with system access. Training must be recorded, refreshed and verified during audits and inspections.
Cybersecurity Workforce Development (Maritime)CYBER
The structured effort to build cyber competence across the maritime workforce — from basic awareness for all seafarers, through role-specific training for masters, engineers and superintendents, to specialist maritime security professionals ashore. Frameworks such as the IMO's cyber risk management guidelines and emerging training standards push companies beyond one-off awareness campaigns toward competence that is assessed, refreshed and tied to real onboard systems. Simulator-based and scenario-based training is increasingly used so crews experience realistic incidents before facing them at sea.

D

Dark WebCYBER
Parts of the internet reachable only through anonymising software such as Tor, where marketplaces and forums operate outside normal search and oversight. Stolen shipping-company credentials, leaked cargo and vessel data, and access to compromised networks are bought and sold there. Many operators subscribe to dark-web monitoring so they learn quickly when their fleet's data or employee logins appear for sale.
Data ClassificationCYBER
Labelling information according to its sensitivity — for example public, internal, confidential and restricted — so people know how each type must be stored, shared and destroyed. In shipping, charter rates, voyage orders, crew medical data and security plans clearly deserve stronger protection than a press release, but without classification everything tends to be treated the same. A simple three- or four-level scheme, applied consistently, is far more useful than an elaborate one nobody follows.
Data DiodeCYBER
A hardware device that physically allows data to travel in one direction only, typically implemented with an optical transmitter on one side and only a receiver on the other. On ships, a diode lets performance and condition data flow from the automation network to shore reporting systems while making it physically impossible for anything — commands, malware, or remote sessions — to travel back into the OT network. It is one of the few controls whose guarantee rests on physics rather than software configuration, which makes it attractive for protecting high-value systems like DP and machinery control.
Data ExfiltrationCYBER
The unauthorised transfer of data out of an organisation's systems to an attacker. In shipping, attractive targets include charter rates, cargo manifests, crew personal data, bills of lading and voyage plans, which can enable fraud, cargo theft or competitive advantage. Exfiltration is often slow and disguised as normal traffic, so unusual outbound data volumes, especially over a vessel's satellite link, are an important warning sign.
DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service)CYBER
An attack in which many compromised machines flood a target with traffic until it becomes unusable. In the maritime sector, DDoS attacks have targeted port community systems, booking platforms and shipping company websites, disrupting cargo operations and communications even though no data is stolen. A vessel with limited satellite bandwidth is also vulnerable, since even a modest traffic flood can saturate the ship-shore link.
Deception TechnologyCYBER
A defensive approach that plants realistic decoys — fake servers, credentials, files or control-system devices — throughout a network to lure attackers into revealing themselves. Any interaction with a decoy is by definition suspicious, so deception generates high-quality alerts with very few false alarms. In an IT-OT environment, a decoy that imitates a PLC or vessel management interface can expose an intruder probing the OT network long before real equipment is touched.
Deepfake FraudCYBER
Fraud using AI-generated audio or video that convincingly imitates a real person's voice or face. In a maritime context, the risk scenario is a finance employee receiving a phone or video call that appears to be the CFO or a ship manager urgently authorising a payment or bunker order. Because the voice alone can no longer be trusted, high-value instructions should be verified through a separate, pre-agreed channel.
Defence in DepthCYBER
The security principle of layering multiple independent controls — perimeter firewalls, network segmentation, access control, monitoring, hardened endpoints and trained people — so that no single compromised layer gives an attacker the whole ship. It is the cyber analogue of the redundancy thinking familiar from DP: assume any single barrier can fail, and design so the failure is contained.
Digital CertificateCYBER
An electronic credential that binds a cryptographic key to an identity — a website, a person, a device or a piece of software — and is vouched for by a trusted certificate authority. Certificates are what let a ship's systems verify they are really talking to the owner's shore servers, and what lets a computer confirm a software update genuinely came from the equipment vendor. They have expiry dates and must be renewed, which is why certificate management matters operationally.
Digital Certificates for e-NavigationCYBER
Cryptographic certificates used to prove the origin and integrity of digital navigation data and services — signed electronic navigational charts, S-100 data products, and authenticated ship-shore data exchange under standards such as IALA's SECOM. The idea is that a bridge system can verify that a chart update or navigational warning genuinely comes from the stated authority and has not been altered in transit. This depends on a trusted infrastructure of certificate authorities for the maritime domain, which bodies including IALA and IHO are actively building out.
Digital ForensicsCYBER
The discipline of collecting and analysing digital evidence — logs, disk images, memory contents, network records — to establish what happened during an incident, without altering the evidence in the process. After a shipboard incident, forensics can determine how attackers got in, what they accessed and whether malware remains. The key operational point for vessel crews: do not wipe, reinstall or "tidy up" affected systems before evidence is preserved, however tempting it is to get things running again quickly.
Digital Twin SecurityCYBER
Securing the data pipelines and shore platforms behind digital twins — live virtual models of a vessel's hull, machinery, or power plant fed by onboard sensors. The twin requires a continuous data feed from the ship, and if that feed is engineered as a two-way channel it becomes another remote path into the OT network. There is also an integrity dimension: decisions about maintenance, fuel efficiency, or operating limits are made from twin data, so tampering with the feed or the model can mislead both shore staff and crew without any alarm on board.
Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)CYBER
The technical companion to the business continuity plan: a documented, step-by-step procedure for restoring IT systems and data after a major failure or attack. It specifies restore order, backup locations, responsible people and target timeframes for each system. For fleet operators it should cover both shore systems and the process for rebuilding shipboard computers, ideally without needing a technician to fly to the vessel. Like backups themselves, a DRP is only trustworthy once it has actually been tested.
Disk EncryptionCYBER
Encrypting the entire contents of a computer's storage so that data is unreadable without the correct key or password. If a laptop is lost in port, or a hard drive is removed from a decommissioned vessel system, disk encryption prevents whoever holds the hardware from reading charts, manifests, crew data or network credentials. It protects data at rest only — once the machine is running and unlocked, other controls must take over.
DLP — Data Loss PreventionCYBER
Tools and policies that detect and block sensitive data leaving the organisation through email, cloud uploads, removable media or messaging. DLP systems inspect content for patterns such as personal data, commercial terms or technical drawings and can warn the user, encrypt the message or stop the transfer. For shipping companies handling charter rates, cargo details and crew records, DLP reduces both accidental leaks and deliberate exfiltration.
DMZ (Demilitarised Zone)CYBER
A buffer network segment placed between two networks of different trust levels, commonly between a ship's business IT network and its OT or automation networks. Servers that must exchange data across the boundary, such as data historians or remote-monitoring gateways, are placed in the DMZ so that no direct connection exists between the internet-facing side and the control systems. This design, recommended in industrial security standards, limits how far an intrusion in the office network can spread towards the engine room.
DNS FilteringCYBER
Blocking access to malicious or unwanted websites by refusing to resolve their domain names — the lookup step every internet connection begins with. Because it works before any content is downloaded, DNS filtering is a lightweight, low-bandwidth control well suited to satellite-connected vessels. It stops many phishing links and malware call-home connections on crew and business networks without inspecting the traffic itself.
DNS SpoofingCYBER
An attack that corrupts the domain name lookup process so users are silently redirected from a legitimate address to an attacker-controlled server. A crew member typing the correct address for a webmail or banking site could land on a convincing fake that harvests credentials. Using secured DNS services and validating certificates helps ships and offices detect the redirection.
Double ExtortionCYBER
A ransomware tactic in which attackers first steal a copy of the victim's data and then encrypt the originals, demanding payment both to restore systems and to prevent publication of the stolen files. For a shipping company this means good backups alone no longer neutralise the threat: leaked charter agreements, crew records or customer data carry their own commercial and regulatory damage. It has become the default model for major ransomware groups targeting the logistics sector.
DP Control Network SecurityCYBER
Protection of the redundant networks linking a dynamic positioning system's controllers, operator stations, position reference systems, and thruster interfaces. DP class rules address failures through redundancy, but redundant networks running identical software can share a single cyber vulnerability, so a worm or corrupted update could take down both DP networks at once — a common-cause failure that redundancy does not protect against. DP networks should therefore be isolated from other shipboard systems, have every external interface (position references, remote diagnostics, update paths) documented and controlled, and any cyber anomaly treated with the same seriousness as a DP alarm.
Drive-By DownloadCYBER
Malware that installs simply by visiting a compromised or malicious web page, without the user clicking or downloading anything, by exploiting browser or plugin vulnerabilities. Crew browsing on shared vessel computers or personal devices connected to ship networks are a realistic exposure route. Keeping browsers patched and separating crew welfare networks from operational systems limits the consequences.
Dwell TimeCYBER
The period between an attacker first gaining access to a network and being detected, historically measured in weeks or months. Long dwell time lets attackers map the fleet IT environment, harvest credentials and position themselves before striking. Reducing dwell time through monitoring and log review is a core measure of a shipping company's detection capability, and vessels with limited connectivity and oversight are where intrusions linger longest.

E

ECDIS Cyber VulnerabilitiesCYBER
Security weaknesses affecting Electronic Chart Display and Information Systems, which are essentially computers running commercial operating systems on the bridge. Typical issues include outdated or unsupported operating systems, chart updates delivered by USB stick, and network connections to other bridge equipment that were never designed with security in mind. Because ECDIS is central to navigation, a compromised unit can display corrupted charts or fail at a critical moment, so units should be hardened, updated through controlled procedures and backed by independent means of position fixing.
EDI Security (Shipping)CYBER
Protecting electronic data interchange — the structured machine-to-machine messages (such as EDIFACT bookings, manifests, bay plans and status updates) that carriers, terminals, agents and customs authorities exchange constantly. These flows were designed decades ago for reliability rather than security, and weakly protected EDI connections or mailboxes can allow attackers to read commercial data or inject fraudulent messages, such as altered container release information. Securing EDI means encrypting the transport, authenticating both endpoints, and validating message content before automated systems act on it.
EDR — Endpoint Detection and ResponseCYBER
A security agent on computers and servers that continuously records what is happening — processes, connections, file changes — and uses that visibility to detect attacks that traditional antivirus misses. EDR also enables response: an analyst ashore can isolate an infected onboard machine from the network or kill a malicious process remotely. On ships, EDR agents must be chosen and tuned for intermittent connectivity so they keep protecting and recording even when the vessel is offline.
Electronic Bill of Lading SecurityCYBER
The protection of electronic bills of lading (eBLs) — digital versions of the document of title that controls who may claim cargo. Because possession of the document effectively equals possession of the goods, an attacker who compromises an eBL platform account or steals its credentials could redirect or fraudulently release cargo worth millions. Security therefore rests on strong user authentication, reliable proof of who holds the document at any moment, and the platform's own integrity — areas addressed by approval schemes for eBL providers and the legal framework of the UNCITRAL MLETR.
Email Authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC)CYBER
Three complementary standards that let receiving mail servers check whether an email genuinely comes from the domain it claims. SPF lists which servers may send for a domain, DKIM adds a cryptographic signature to each message, and DMARC tells receivers what to do when checks fail and reports abuse attempts back to the domain owner. For shipping companies — frequent targets of invoice fraud and charter-party scams impersonating owners, agents and bunker suppliers — publishing strict records for these standards makes their domains far harder to spoof.
Email Security GatewayCYBER
A filtering service that inspects inbound and outbound email for phishing, malicious attachments, spoofed senders and spam before messages reach users. Since email remains the most common way attackers reach shipping companies — fake charterer invoices, spoofed port authority messages, malware-laden attachments — the gateway is one of the highest-value defensive controls a fleet can deploy. Modern gateways also rewrite suspicious links and sandbox attachments before delivery.
EncryptionCYBER
The process of encoding data so that only authorised parties holding the correct key can read it. On ships, encryption protects data moving over satellite links, remote-access sessions to onboard systems, and stored information such as crew records and cargo documentation. Legacy shipboard protocols, including many used by navigation and automation equipment, transmit data unencrypted, which is one reason network segregation matters so much on board.
Encryption at RestCYBER
Encrypting data where it is stored — on laptops, servers, backup drives and cloud storage — so that it remains unreadable if the hardware is stolen or the storage is accessed without authorisation. It matters in shipping because vessels and offices hold sensitive material on portable, physically exposed equipment: crew personal data, cargo documentation, voyage and commercial records. Modern operating systems and cloud services make it largely a matter of switching the capability on and, critically, managing the recovery keys properly.
Encryption in TransitCYBER
Encrypting data while it moves between systems — most commonly with TLS, the protocol behind HTTPS, or through encrypted VPN tunnels. For ships this protects traffic over satellite and shore links, which may pass through many networks and providers on the way: without it, credentials, operational messages and commercial data can be intercepted or altered en route. It is standard, low-cost practice ashore, and any ship-shore application or vendor connection still using unencrypted protocols should be treated as a finding to fix.
End-to-End EncryptionCYBER
Encryption arranged so that only the communicating endpoints — for example two messaging apps, or a ship system and its shore counterpart — can read the content, with no intermediate server or provider able to decrypt it. This differs from ordinary encryption in transit, where data is often decrypted and re-encrypted at servers along the way. It suits highly sensitive exchanges such as incident communications or commercially critical negotiations, with the trade-off that the intermediary cannot scan content for malware or retain it for compliance purposes.
Endpoint ProtectionCYBER
Security software installed on individual devices, such as bridge PCs, planning stations and office laptops, that detects and blocks malware and suspicious behaviour. Modern endpoint protection goes beyond signature-based antivirus, monitoring how programs behave and reporting alerts to fleet security teams ashore. On vessels with intermittent connectivity, tools must be able to operate and update sensibly despite long offline periods.
Engine Control System SecurityCYBER
Protection of the electronic systems that govern main and auxiliary engines — governor and fuel injection control, safety shutdowns, and the bridge and engine control room remote-control chain. Modern engines are software-defined machines whose behaviour can be altered through service laptops, OEM remote links, and parameter files, making the maintenance and diagnostics path the main attack surface. Loss or manipulation of engine control at the wrong moment means loss of propulsion, so access to engine controllers should be as tightly managed as access to the engine room itself.
ENISACYBER
The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, which supports EU member states and industry with cyber security policy, certification frameworks and sector guidance. ENISA has published analyses and good-practice guidance for the maritime sector, including port and shipping cyber security, and plays a role in implementing EU legislation such as NIS2 and EU-wide certification schemes. For operators in Europe, ENISA publications are a useful, authority-backed reference when building or benchmarking a maritime cyber programme.
EU Cyber Resilience ActCYBER
An EU regulation imposing cyber security requirements on products with digital elements placed on the EU market, covering secure design, vulnerability handling and security updates through the product's support period. For maritime suppliers it affects equipment such as network devices, software and connected sensors sold into the EU, complementing sector rules like IACS UR E27. Shipowners benefit indirectly: over time, equipment purchased for vessels should arrive with better security baselines and documented update support.
ExploitCYBER
Code or a technique that takes advantage of a specific software vulnerability to make a system do something its owner did not intend, such as run attacker code or grant access. Exploits exist for everything from office software to the ageing operating systems still found on some bridge and engine-room equipment. Patching removes the vulnerability an exploit depends on, which is why patch management features prominently in maritime cyber guidelines.
Exploit KitCYBER
A packaged toolset, typically hosted on a malicious web page, that automatically probes a visitor's browser and software for known vulnerabilities and fires the matching exploit. Exploit kits industrialise attacks: no skill is needed to use them, and unpatched machines are compromised on contact. Office PCs and shared vessel computers running outdated software are the natural victims.

F

Federated Fleet Data SharingCYBER
Approaches that let multiple shipping companies gain collective insight — benchmarking, emissions analysis, or jointly trained machine-learning models — without handing their raw operational data to a central party. In federated learning, for instance, models are trained locally on each operator's data and only the model updates are shared, so commercially sensitive voyage and performance records stay with their owners. The security questions shift to governing the shared results, since even aggregated outputs can leak competitive information, and to trusting the platform that coordinates the exchange.
Fileless MalwareCYBER
Malicious code that operates in memory and abuses legitimate system tools rather than writing traditional files to disk, making it hard for classic antivirus to spot. Because many vessels rely on signature-based antivirus with infrequent updates, fileless techniques are particularly effective afloat. Detection depends on watching behaviour, such as unusual use of scripting tools, rather than scanning for known files.
FirewallCYBER
A device or software that filters network traffic according to defined rules, permitting legitimate communication and blocking everything else. On board, firewalls sit between the satellite router and the ship's networks, and between IT and OT segments, controlling what may pass between crew Wi-Fi, business systems and control networks. A firewall is only as good as its rule set, so rules should be documented, reviewed and stripped of temporary vendor exceptions that were never removed.
Firmware IntegrityCYBER
Assurance that the low-level software running inside shipboard devices — PLCs, sensors, radios, network switches, engine controllers — is genuine and has not been tampered with. Malicious or corrupted firmware sits below the operating system, survives reboots, and is invisible to conventional antivirus, making it a persistent foothold in OT equipment. Practical defences at sea include accepting firmware only from verified OEM sources, checking digital signatures or checksums before installation, recording firmware versions in the OT asset inventory, and physically controlling the service ports through which firmware is loaded.
Flag State Cyber RequirementsCYBER
The cyber security expectations a flag administration places on ships flying its flag, implemented through circulars, inspection campaigns and safety management oversight. Most flags require cyber risk to be addressed in the safety management system in line with IMO resolutions, and some — notably the United States through the USCG cybersecurity rule — impose detailed national requirements such as designated officers, plans and incident reporting. Operators with multi-flag fleets must track each flag's circulars, since scope and deadlines differ.
Fleet Cyber Operations CentreCYBER
A shore-based function that continuously monitors the security of vessel networks across a fleet, watching for suspicious traffic, failed logins, malware alerts and unexpected connections on both IT and OT systems. It may be run in-house or bought as a managed service from a maritime security provider, with data fed ashore over the vessels' satellite links. The value lies in spotting an intrusion in hours rather than months, and in giving ships expert backup when something looks wrong — crews are watchkeepers, not security analysts.

G

Gap Analysis (Cyber)CYBER
A structured comparison of a company's or vessel's current cyber security arrangements against a target reference — such as IACS UR E26/E27, the USCG rule, a class notation or ISO/IEC 27001 — to identify what is missing. The output is a prioritised list of gaps with owners, costs and deadlines, which becomes the implementation roadmap. Gap analyses are typically the first step before certification, notation surveys or compliance with new regulation.
GDPR in Maritime OperationsCYBER
The application of the EU General Data Protection Regulation to personal data handled in shipping — crew records, medical information, passenger manifests, CCTV on board and seafarer data exchanged with agents, ports and manning offices. Shipping companies established in the EU/EEA, or handling EU residents' data, must have a lawful basis for processing, protect the data appropriately and report qualifying breaches, which links GDPR duties directly to shipboard and shore cyber security. Data transfers across many jurisdictions during a voyage make careful mapping of processing especially important in maritime operations.
GMDSS Cyber ConsiderationsCYBER
Cyber security aspects of the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System — the MF/HF and VHF radios, satellite terminals, and alerting equipment a ship relies on in an emergency. Newer GMDSS installations are software-based, receive firmware updates, and may share bridge networks with other equipment, so they inherit the same update-path and network risks as any connected device. Because GMDSS is the last line of communication when everything else fails, its equipment should sit on protected navigation/radio networks and its update and interfacing arrangements should be deliberately conservative.
GNSS Resilience (eLoran, R-Mode)CYBER
The ability of a ship to keep navigating and timing accurately when satellite positioning (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou) is jammed, spoofed, or unavailable. Resilience measures include terrestrial backup systems such as eLoran and R-Mode (ranging signals embedded in existing radio beacons and AIS transmissions), multi-constellation and multi-frequency receivers, inertial sensors, and disciplined use of radar and visual fixes. For crews, the practical skill is recognising GNSS degradation quickly — position jumps, unusual HDOP, mismatched sensor positions — and knowing which onboard systems, from ECDIS to DP, silently depend on satellite position and time.
GPS Jamming & SpoofingCYBER
Deliberate interference with satellite navigation: jamming drowns the signal so receivers lose position, while spoofing transmits counterfeit signals that make receivers report a false position. Both are increasingly observed in real operations and are a direct threat to navigation and DP alike — which is why DP redundancy requires position references on different measurement principles, and why bridge teams train to detect and cross-check anomalous positions.

H

HacktivismCYBER
Hacking motivated by political or ideological goals rather than profit, typically aiming for disruption, defacement or embarrassing data leaks. Shipping companies can be targeted over environmental issues, cargoes carried, flags flown or trade with sanctioned or contested regions. Attacks are often opportunistic and noisy, such as website defacement or leaked documents, but timing frequently coincides with geopolitical events.
HardeningCYBER
The process of reducing a system's vulnerability by removing unnecessary software, closing unused ports, disabling default accounts and applying secure configuration settings. Shipboard equipment often arrives from the yard or vendor with default passwords and services enabled that the vessel will never use; hardening strips these away before the system enters service. Class societies and standards such as IACS UR E27 increasingly expect onboard systems to be delivered and maintained in a hardened state.
HashingCYBER
A mathematical operation that converts any data into a short, fixed-length fingerprint; even a one-bit change in the input produces a completely different hash. Hashes are used to verify that files have not been tampered with — for example, checking that a software update for an onboard system matches the vendor's published hash before installation. They also protect stored passwords, since systems keep the hash rather than the password itself. Unlike encryption, hashing is one-way and cannot be reversed.
HMI — Human-Machine InterfaceCYBER
The screens and operator stations through which crew monitor and control machinery, cargo, ballast, and automation systems — typically Windows-based workstations in the engine control room, bridge, and cargo office. Because HMIs run commodity operating systems, accept USB devices, and hold operator credentials, they are usually the easiest entry point into an otherwise closed control network. An attacker who controls the HMI controls what the operator sees, so protecting these stations — hardening, port control, no browsing or email, screen-lock discipline — protects the crew's entire picture of the plant.
HoneypotCYBER
A deliberately exposed decoy system designed to attract attackers and record their methods, with no legitimate users of its own. Because nobody should ever connect to it, any activity on a honeypot is a reliable sign of scanning or intrusion. Researchers have used honeypots imitating ship systems to demonstrate how quickly internet-exposed maritime equipment gets probed; within a company network, a honeypot serves as an early-warning tripwire.
Human Factor in CybersecurityCYBER
The recognition that most cyber incidents involve human behaviour somewhere in the chain — a clicked link, a shared password, a bypassed procedure — and that this is a design problem, not just a discipline problem. Maritime professionals will recognise the parallel with human factors in bridge and engine room operations: fatigue, workload, unclear procedures and poor system design shape behaviour more than posters do. Effective programmes make the secure way the easy way, rather than blaming individuals for predictable errors.

I

IACS UR E26 / UR E27CYBER
Two Unified Requirements from the International Association of Classification Societies that make cyber resilience part of class rules. UR E26 addresses the cyber resilience of the ship as a whole, covering how onboard networks are designed, protected, monitored and recoverable, while UR E27 sets security requirements for individual onboard systems and equipment supplied by manufacturers. Both are mandatory for new ships contracted on or after 1 July 2024, which means shipowners, yards and equipment vendors must now demonstrate cyber security measures to obtain and maintain class.
IAM — Identity and Access ManagementCYBER
The overall framework of policies and systems that governs who a user is, what they may access, and how that access is granted, changed and removed. IAM ties together accounts, authentication, roles and approval workflows across shore offices and vessels. In a fleet context it answers questions such as: what happens to a superintendent's access when they change roles, and how is a rotating crew member's account created and retired? Weak IAM — orphaned accounts, shared logins — is a common root cause in maritime incidents.
IEC 61162-460CYBER
An international standard defining security requirements for shipboard networks that interconnect navigation and radiocommunication equipment, commonly called the "460-network". It specifies how such a network must be protected when connected to other onboard systems or external sources, including requirements for gateways, network monitoring and controlled uncontrolled-network access. Bridge system integrators use compliance with IEC 61162-460 to demonstrate that ECDIS, radar and related equipment sit on a suitably protected network.
IEC 62443CYBER
An international series of standards for the security of industrial automation and control systems, widely referenced in maritime OT security work and underpinning parts of IACS UR E26 and E27. Its core concepts include dividing plant networks into zones and conduits, assigning security levels to each zone, and defining requirements for both product suppliers and system integrators. For a ship, this translates into structured segregation of bridge, machinery and cargo control networks with defined, controlled connections between them.
Immutable BackupCYBER
A backup copy that cannot be altered or deleted for a defined retention period, even by an administrator. This matters because modern ransomware deliberately seeks out and encrypts or deletes backups before triggering, leaving victims with nothing to restore. Immutable storage — whether cloud-based object locks or write-once media — guarantees a clean recovery point survives, which for a shipping company can mean the difference between a day of downtime and rebuilding fleet operations from scratch.
IMO Guidelines MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3CYBER
The IMO's Guidelines on Maritime Cyber Risk Management, issued jointly by the Maritime Safety Committee and Facilitation Committee and updated through subsequent revisions. They set out high-level functional elements — identify, protect, detect, respond and recover — that administrations and companies should apply to both information technology and operational technology on ships. The guidelines are the reference framework behind the IMO's requirement to address cyber risk in safety management systems, and most flag and industry guidance traces back to them.
Incident Response PlanCYBER
A documented, rehearsed procedure describing what ship and shore staff must do when a cyber incident occurs: how to recognise it, whom to notify, how to isolate affected systems and how to keep the vessel operating safely. A good shipboard plan assumes the incident may happen mid-ocean with limited connectivity, and gives the bridge and engine-room teams clear fallback procedures, such as reverting to manual control or paper charts. Like fire and abandon-ship drills, the plan only works if it is exercised, so cyber scenarios should be included in the vessel's drill programme.
Industrial Control System (ICS)CYBER
The umbrella term for the hardware and software that monitor and control physical processes — controllers, sensors, actuators, operator stations, and the networks connecting them. On a ship, ICS covers propulsion and power management, steering, ballast, cargo handling, and HVAC, usually delivered by several different OEMs. ICS security differs from office IT security because the priority order reverses: keeping the process running safely comes first, and conventional IT measures such as frequent patching or automatic reboots can themselves endanger the vessel if applied carelessly.
Information Sharing & Analysis Centre (ISAC)CYBER
A membership organisation through which companies in one sector share threat intelligence, incident experience and defensive advice in a trusted setting. Maritime examples include Norway's NORMA Cyber, which serves the Norwegian shipping community, and MTS-ISAC in the United States for the marine transportation system. The value is early warning: an attack technique seen at one operator — a phishing theme, a fraudulent charterer, a vulnerability being exploited — reaches the rest of the membership before it hits them, in a forum where incidents can be discussed without publicity.
Initial AccessCYBER
The first foothold an attacker establishes inside a target network, the beachhead from which everything else follows. In shipping the common routes are phishing emails to shore staff, stolen or reused credentials on remote-access services, exposed internet-facing systems, and infected USB media or laptops brought aboard by crew and contractors. Hardening these entry points delivers the most defensive value per effort.
Initial Access BrokerCYBER
A criminal who specialises in breaking into organisations and then sells that access on underground markets to other criminals, often ransomware groups. Division of labour means a shipping company may be breached by one actor and attacked weeks later by a completely different one who bought the way in. Access to logistics and maritime firms is traded like any commodity, priced by company size and access level.
Insider ThreatCYBER
The risk that a crew member, shore employee, contractor or vendor technician misuses legitimate access to harm the ship or company, whether deliberately or through carelessness. On vessels, riding crews and service engineers routinely receive deep access to control systems with little oversight, and a disgruntled or coerced insider can bypass many technical defences. Mitigations include least-privilege access, logging of privileged actions, supervision of third-party work and a culture in which unusual behaviour can be reported without stigma.
Integrated Automation System (IAS)CYBER
The vessel-wide control platform that brings machinery, power, ballast, cargo, and auxiliary systems together into one alarm, monitoring, and control environment, typically with operator stations in the engine control room, on the bridge, and in the cargo office. The IAS is the natural aggregation point of the ship's OT — and therefore its highest-value cyber target, since compromising it grants visibility and often control over most of the plant. Its redundant servers, field networks, and any links to administrative networks or shore support should be documented, segregated, and monitored as the core of the ship's cyber risk assessment.
Integrated Bridge System (IBS) SecurityCYBER
Protection of the interconnected navigation suite — ECDIS, radar, autopilot, conning display, GNSS, gyro, log, and echo sounder — that shares data across common bridge networks. Integration improves the watchkeeper's picture but also means a single compromised component or poisoned sensor feed can propagate bad data to every display at once. IBS security relies on keeping the navigation network isolated from business and crew networks (this is what the 460-network standard formalises), controlling every maintenance and update interface, and training officers to cross-check integrated displays against independent sources.
Intrusion Detection and Prevention (IDS/IPS)CYBER
Technology that monitors network traffic for signs of attack; an intrusion detection system (IDS) raises alerts, while an intrusion prevention system (IPS) can also block the offending traffic automatically. On ships, passive detection is often preferred on OT networks, because automatically blocking traffic on an automation or DP network could itself disrupt a safety-critical function. Alerts are typically forwarded over the satellite link to a shore-side security team for analysis.
ISO/IEC 27001CYBER
The international standard for information security management systems, defining how an organisation identifies risks, applies controls and continually improves its security posture. Shipping companies certify their shore organisations against ISO/IEC 27001 to demonstrate structured security governance to charterers, insurers and authorities, and its risk-based approach pairs naturally with the ISM Code's safety management system. It concerns the management framework rather than specific shipboard technology, so it complements rather than replaces OT-focused standards such as IEC 62443.
ISO/IEC 27002CYBER
An international standard providing a reference catalogue of information security controls and implementation guidance, used alongside ISO/IEC 27001. Where 27001 defines the management system that a shipping company can be certified against, 27002 describes the individual controls — access control, cryptography, supplier security, incident management and others — in practical detail. Maritime IT departments typically use 27002 as the menu from which controls in the company's Statement of Applicability are selected and tailored to fleet operations.
ISO/IEC 27005CYBER
An international standard giving guidance on information security risk management: establishing context, identifying and analysing risks, evaluating them against criteria and treating them. In a maritime setting it provides the method behind cybersecurity assessments — for example, how to rate the likelihood and consequence of a compromised planned maintenance system versus a spoofed navigation feed. It supports ISO/IEC 27001 but is method-oriented and can be applied even by companies not seeking certification.
ISPS–Cyber InterfaceCYBER
The overlap between the ISPS Code's ship and port facility security regime and cyber security management. Ship Security Assessments and Ship Security Plans increasingly need to consider cyber threats to security-related systems — access control, CCTV, AIS and communications — and several administrations expect cyber to be addressed within or alongside ISPS documentation. Aligning the CSO/SSO structure with cyber roles avoids gaps where a threat is neither clearly "physical security" nor clearly "IT".
IT/OT ConvergenceCYBER
The growing interconnection of information technology (business systems, email, planning tools) with operational technology (engine automation, navigation, cargo control) on modern vessels. Remote engine monitoring, performance optimisation and predictive maintenance all depend on OT data flowing ashore through IT networks, which erodes the isolation that once protected control systems. Convergence brings real operational benefits, but it means a phishing email opened in the ship's office can now be the first step towards an incident in the engine room, so the boundary between the two worlds must be deliberately designed and monitored.

J

Joiner-Mover-Leaver ProcessCYBER
The procedure for granting, adjusting and revoking system access as people join the company, change roles or leave. In shipping this must cope with constant crew rotation: accounts and access rights for seafarers signing on and off, superintendents changing fleets, and third parties such as agency crew or riding squads. The most common failure is the "leaver" step — accounts of departed employees or contractors that stay active for months are a classic way in for attackers.
Just Culture (Cyber Reporting)CYBER
An organisational culture in which people can report mistakes and near-misses — including clicking a phishing link or plugging in the wrong USB stick — without fear of punishment, while genuine recklessness is still addressed. Shipping adopted this thinking from safety management, and it applies directly to cyber: an officer who reports a suspicious email immediately gives the company hours of head start, while one who stays silent out of fear gives the attackers days. If the first person to report a mistake gets blamed, it will also be the last time anyone reports one.

K

Key ManagementCYBER
The processes and tools for generating, distributing, storing, rotating and retiring cryptographic keys throughout their life. Encryption is only as strong as the protection of its keys: a key stored in a spreadsheet or hard-coded into an onboard application undermines everything built on it. Good practice keeps keys in dedicated hardware or key-management services, limits who can use them, and ensures keys can be rotated without breaking ship-shore communications.
KeyloggerCYBER
Software or a small hardware device that secretly records every keystroke, capturing passwords, messages and financial details as they are typed. A keylogger on a shared vessel computer or an operations workstation ashore can quietly harvest logins for email, banking and fleet management systems over weeks. Hardware variants plugged between keyboard and computer are a reason to physically inspect publicly accessible machines.

L

Lateral MovementCYBER
The techniques attackers use to spread from their initial foothold to other systems in the network, hopping between machines while hunting for valuable targets. In a fleet context this is how a compromise of one office PC can eventually reach the crewing database, the finance system, or, where networks are poorly separated, a vessel's onboard systems. Network segmentation, including the ship-shore boundary and the IT-OT split on board, exists largely to stop lateral movement.
Least PrivilegeCYBER
The principle that every user, application and system should have only the minimum access rights needed to do its job, and nothing more. On board, this means deck cadets do not get administrator rights on bridge PCs, the crew Wi-Fi cannot reach the automation network, and vendor accounts are enabled only for the duration of a service visit. Least privilege limits the damage any single compromised account or device can cause.
Legacy Systems (OT)CYBER
Shipboard control equipment that remains in service long after its software stopped receiving security support — automation stations running Windows XP or 7, controllers with unpatchable firmware, and protocols designed with no security at all. Ships routinely operate for 25 years or more while OT vendors support software for far less, so nearly every vessel carries systems that cannot be patched or hardened directly. The realistic strategy is compensating controls: isolate legacy systems behind firewalls or diodes, remove unnecessary connectivity, restrict physical access, monitor their traffic passively, and plan replacement at dry-docking rather than pretending the risk does not exist.
LEO Connectivity Security (Starlink-Class)CYBER
Security implications of high-bandwidth, low-latency internet from low-Earth-orbit constellations, which many fleets now install alongside or instead of traditional VSAT. The step change in bandwidth changes the threat model: shore-style attacks, large malware downloads, and always-on remote sessions become practical against ships that were previously protected partly by their own poor connectivity. LEO terminals are often installed quickly and outside the established VSAT management regime, so the priority is bringing them under the same firewall, monitoring, and change-control arrangements as every other WAN link — and deciding deliberately which onboard networks may reach them.
Lessons Learned ProcessCYBER
A structured review held after an incident, drill or exercise to capture what worked, what did not, and what should change — then actually making those changes. Shipping companies already do this for safety incidents under the ISM Code; the same discipline applies after a cyber event or exercise. The output should be concrete: updated procedures, revised contact lists, new training content or technical fixes, each with an owner and deadline, rather than a report that gets filed and forgotten.
Living off the LandCYBER
An attacker strategy of using tools already present on the victim's systems, such as built-in administration utilities and scripting engines, instead of installing recognisable malware. Because the activity looks like routine IT administration, it blends in, especially in environments with little monitoring, such as many vessel networks. Defending against it means restricting powerful built-in tools and flagging when they are used at odd times or by odd accounts.
Logic BombCYBER
Malicious code hidden inside a system that stays dormant until a specific trigger, such as a date, an event, or the deletion of a particular user account, then executes its payload. The classic scenario is a disgruntled employee or contractor planting one before departure. For shipping companies this argues for reviewing changes made by departing IT staff and vendors with privileged access to fleet or vessel systems.

M

MalwareCYBER
A general term for malicious software, including viruses, worms, trojans, spyware and ransomware, designed to disrupt systems, steal data or give attackers control. Ships are exposed through email attachments, infected USB sticks, vendor laptops and downloads over crew internet, and malware has reached ECDIS units and cargo systems in documented incidents. Layered defences, endpoint protection, controlled media handling and crew awareness together reduce the likelihood that malware reaches critical shipboard systems.
Man-in-the-Middle AttackCYBER
An attack where the adversary secretly positions themselves between two communicating parties, able to read and alter traffic while both sides believe they are talking directly to each other. Maritime scenarios include intercepting traffic on untrusted port Wi-Fi used by crew, or tampering with ship-shore data exchanges that lack encryption. Properly implemented encryption (such as TLS and VPNs) is the primary defence.
Management of Change (OT)CYBER
A formal process for assessing and approving modifications to operational technology — engine automation, power management, DP systems, cargo control — before they are made. Because OT changes can affect vessel safety and class approval, the process typically involves the technical superintendent, the system vendor and sometimes class, not just IT. It covers software updates, new remote access connections, replaced components and network modifications, and ensures each change is risk-assessed, documented, and reversible if something goes wrong at sea.
Maritime CERT/CSIRT EcosystemCYBER
The network of computer emergency response teams (CERTs) and computer security incident response teams (CSIRTs) that support the maritime sector when incidents occur. It spans national CERTs, sector bodies such as NORMA Cyber's operational response function, commercial incident-response providers, and internal teams at larger shipping companies, with EU rules under NIS2 formalising incident reporting for many operators. For a shipping company the practical question is knowing before an incident which team it would call, what that team can actually do, and which authorities must be notified within what deadlines.
Maritime Cyber RangeCYBER
A controlled training environment that simulates ship and port systems — bridge equipment, automation, communication links and business networks — so that realistic cyber attacks can be launched and defended against without touching a real vessel. Ranges are used to train crews and shore staff, exercise incident-response plans, and test how attacks on navigation or machinery systems actually unfold. Several maritime nations, including Norway, have invested in such facilities because hands-on experience of an unfolding incident builds competence that classroom awareness training cannot.
Maritime Single Window (Security Aspects)CYBER
The national digital platform through which ships submit port call information once, electronically — mandatory for FAL Convention reporting since 2024. Because the single window concentrates commercially and security-sensitive data (crew lists, cargo details, arrival times) and connects ships, agents and authorities, it is an attractive cyber target, and its availability directly affects port clearance. Operators should treat credentials and interfaces to single-window systems as part of their cyber risk assessment, and states are expected to secure the platforms accordingly.
Maritime Testbeds (Cyber)CYBER
Physical or virtual facilities where maritime systems and new technologies can be security-tested under realistic conditions — from laboratory rigs with real bridge and automation equipment to designated sea areas used for trialling autonomous vessels. They allow researchers and vendors to probe equipment for vulnerabilities, validate defences and rehearse attack scenarios legally and safely, which is rarely possible on operational ships. Norwegian test areas for autonomous shipping, such as the Trondheimsfjord, combine this with operational trials, linking cyber assurance to real approval processes.
MDR — Managed Detection and ResponseCYBER
A subscription service in which an external provider's analysts monitor your systems around the clock, investigate alerts and act on genuine threats on your behalf. MDR effectively rents the 24/7 security operations capability that most shipowners and managers cannot justify building in-house. For fleets, the key selection criteria are whether the provider understands OT and vessel environments and how response actions are coordinated with the ship when it is at sea.
Media Sanitisation & DisposalCYBER
Securely erasing or destroying data storage before equipment leaves the company's control — whether it is being sold, scrapped, returned to a vendor or landed from a vessel. Simply deleting files or formatting a drive does not remove the data; proper sanitisation means verified wiping or physical destruction. This matters at ship recycling and equipment replacement: old ECDIS units, loading computers and office PCs can carry voyage data, charter information and crew records long after the ship changes hands.
MicrosegmentationCYBER
Dividing a network into very fine-grained zones — down to individual systems or workloads — with explicit rules governing what may talk to what. Where traditional segmentation might separate "IT" from "OT", microsegmentation can specify that only the DP historian may query the DP controllers, on one protocol, in one direction. This sharply limits how far an intruder or worm can spread laterally after an initial foothold, though it demands accurate knowledge of legitimate traffic flows to implement without breaking operations.
MITRE ATT&CKCYBER
A publicly available knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques based on real-world observations, maintained by MITRE, with a dedicated matrix for industrial control systems (ATT&CK for ICS). Maritime security teams use it to structure threat analysis, map detections and design realistic drill scenarios — for example, tracing how an attacker could move from a shore email compromise to shipboard OT. Class societies, consultants and incident responders use ATT&CK terminology as a common language when describing attacks on vessel systems.
ModbusCYBER
A simple industrial protocol from the 1970s, still widely used aboard ships to connect PLCs, power management systems, switchboards, and sensors, either over serial lines or Ethernet (Modbus TCP). Modbus has no authentication or encryption: any device that can reach a Modbus port can read values and write commands, and the protocol will obey. Since the protocol itself cannot be secured, defence consists of strict network segmentation, firewall rules that restrict who may talk Modbus to which device, and monitoring for write commands from unexpected sources.
Multi-Factor AuthenticationCYBER
A login method requiring two or more independent proofs of identity, typically something you know (a password) combined with something you have (a phone app or hardware token) or something you are (a fingerprint). MFA dramatically reduces the impact of stolen or phished passwords, and is particularly important for remote access to vessels, fleet management portals and company email. At sea, solutions must account for limited connectivity, for example by using hardware tokens or offline code generators rather than SMS.

N

National Cybersecurity Authorities (e.g. NSM Norway)CYBER
Government bodies responsible for national cyber security policy, guidance and incident coordination, such as the Norwegian National Security Authority (NSM), which publishes baseline security principles widely used by Norwegian maritime companies. These authorities issue advisories, receive incident notifications under national law and often operate the national CERT that ships' companies can contact during serious incidents. Maritime operators should know which authority is competent in their flag and operating states, since obligations under regimes like NIS2 are enforced nationally.
Network Access Control (NAC)CYBER
Technology that decides whether a device may join a network at all, based on its identity, health and authorisation — and quarantines or blocks anything unknown. On a vessel, NAC prevents a contractor's laptop, a crew phone or a rogue device plugged into a spare switch port from silently gaining access to operational networks. It typically checks certificates or credentials at the switch or Wi-Fi level and assigns each device to the appropriate network zone automatically.
Network SegmentationCYBER
Dividing a ship's network into separate zones, typically crew, business and OT/control networks, with firewalls or other controls governing traffic between them. Segmentation ensures that a compromise of the crew Wi-Fi or an infected office PC cannot spread directly to navigation, automation or cargo systems. It is a cornerstone of maritime cyber guidance and of IACS UR E26, and it should be verified in practice, since undocumented cross-connections and forgotten cables often undermine the design on paper.
Network Traffic Analysis / NetFlowCYBER
Monitoring the patterns of network communication — who talks to whom, when, how much and on which protocols — to spot anomalies without necessarily inspecting content. NetFlow and similar records are compact enough to collect even over satellite links, making them practical for vessel networks. Because OT traffic aboard ship is highly repetitive and predictable, a new connection from an engine-room device to the internet, or unusual data volumes leaving the vessel, stands out clearly.
Next-Generation Firewall (NGFW)CYBER
A firewall that goes beyond blocking by address and port: it identifies the actual applications in the traffic, inspects content for threats, and can apply rules per user or device. On a vessel, an NGFW at the boundary between business and OT networks can permit exactly the vendor application needed for remote diagnostics while blocking everything else, even if it uses the same port. NGFWs commonly bundle intrusion prevention, web filtering and VPN termination in one appliance, which suits space- and power-constrained shipboard installations.
NIS2 DirectiveCYBER
The EU directive on network and information security that has applied since October 2024, replacing the original NIS Directive and covering many maritime transport companies, ports and related services as essential or important entities. It requires in-scope organisations to implement risk-based cyber security measures, manage supply-chain risk, report significant incidents to national authorities and hold management accountable for compliance. For shipping companies operating in or serving EU member states, NIS2 adds enforceable legal obligations on top of the IMO's SMS-based cyber requirements.
NIST Cybersecurity FrameworkCYBER
A voluntary framework from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology that organises cyber security work into core functions — govern, identify, protect, detect, respond and recover. The IMO's maritime cyber risk guidelines follow essentially the same functional structure, so many shipping companies use the NIST framework as the backbone of their cyber risk management and map class, flag and vetting requirements onto it. It is technology-neutral and scales from a single vessel operator to a large fleet.
NIST SP 800-82CYBER
A NIST special publication providing guidance on securing operational technology (OT) — the control systems that on ships include propulsion control, power management, cargo and ballast systems. It explains how OT differs from ordinary IT (availability and safety come first, patching is constrained) and recommends architectures, segmentation and countermeasures suited to control environments. Maritime OT engineers and consultants use it alongside IEC 62443 when designing or reviewing shipboard automation security.
NMEA 0183CYBER
The long-standing serial standard by which navigation instruments — GPS, gyro, log, echo sounder, AIS — exchange plain-text sentences with ECDIS, radar, autopilot, and VDR. The sentences carry only a simple checksum for transmission errors, with no authentication, so any device or converter with access to the line can inject fabricated position, heading, or speed data that receiving equipment will trust completely. Because most bridge equipment still speaks NMEA 0183, protecting these unglamorous serial links and the Ethernet converters that carry them is a core part of bridge system security.
NMEA 2000CYBER
A CAN-based network standard that connects navigation and engine instruments over a single backbone cable, common on workboats, fishing vessels, and yachts, and increasingly on larger ships for sensor networks. Like its CAN foundation, NMEA 2000 has no device authentication: every node on the backbone is trusted, and researchers have repeatedly demonstrated injection of false GNSS, depth, and engine data by attaching a rogue device. Security therefore comes down to physical control of the backbone, knowing every device on it, and being cautious with gateway products that bridge NMEA 2000 to Wi-Fi or onboard Ethernet.

O

OCIMF SIRE 2.0 (Cyber Aspects)CYBER
The current OCIMF tanker inspection regime, which replaced the previous SIRE programme with a risk-based, digital question set — including questions on cyber security awareness, procedures and hardware on board. Inspectors may ask crew how removable media, network access and suspicious emails are handled, and observe whether bridge and engine room systems are protected in practice. Poor cyber answers now affect vetting outcomes for tanker operators, making SIRE 2.0 a commercial driver for shipboard cyber discipline.
Offline / Offsite BackupCYBER
Backup copies kept disconnected from the network (offline) or stored in a different physical location (offsite), so they survive events that destroy the primary systems. An offline copy — a rotated external drive locked in the ship's office, for instance — cannot be reached by ransomware spreading across the network. An offsite copy, typically replicated ashore, survives loss of the vessel's equipment entirely. Most fleet backup strategies combine both, alongside regular test restores to prove the copies actually work.
Onboard Network TopologyCYBER
The actual layout of a ship's networks — navigation, automation, safety, administrative, and crew — including every switch, firewall, wireless access point, serial converter, and satellite or cellular gateway. An accurate, maintained topology drawing is the foundation of shipboard cyber security: without it, segmentation cannot be verified, incidents cannot be traced, and undocumented links installed during retrofits or service visits go unnoticed. In practice the as-built topology often differs from the yard drawings, so periodic verification against reality — walking the racks and tracing cables — is a genuine security control, not paperwork.
OPC UACYBER
A modern industrial communication standard (OPC Unified Architecture) used to exchange data between control systems and higher-level applications, such as feeding IAS data to performance monitoring or shore reporting platforms. Unlike legacy OT protocols, OPC UA was designed with security built in — certificate-based authentication, signing, and encryption — but these features only protect the ship if they are actually enabled, since many installations run in anonymous, unencrypted mode for convenience. When commissioning OPC UA links on board, verifying that security policies are enforced and certificates are properly managed is the difference between a secured interface and an open data tap.
Operational Technology (OT)CYBER
The hardware and software that monitors and controls physical processes on board: engine and propulsion automation, power management, dynamic positioning, steering, ballast, cargo and safety systems. Unlike IT, where confidentiality usually matters most, OT security prioritises availability and integrity, because a corrupted sensor value or frozen controller can directly endanger the vessel. OT systems often run for decades on legacy software that cannot simply be patched or rebooted at sea, which is why they demand different security strategies from office IT.
OT Asset InventoryCYBER
A maintained register of every control and monitoring device on board — controllers, HMIs, network equipment, sensors, gateways — with details such as location, OEM, software and firmware versions, network addresses, and external connections. It is the starting point of any credible shipboard cyber programme, because systems that are not known cannot be protected, patched, or monitored; undocumented service modems and retrofit gateways are found on most vessels the first time an inventory is done properly. The inventory should be kept current through the ship's management-of-change process and used actively when assessing new vulnerabilities against the fleet.
OT Cybersecurity Skills GapCYBER
The shortage of people who understand both operational technology — the control systems running propulsion, power, cargo and navigation — and cybersecurity. IT security specialists rarely know how a ship's automation or DP system behaves, while marine engineers rarely have security training, and the overlap is scarce across the whole industry. The gap shows up in practice as OT risks being assessed with IT assumptions, or not at all; closing it requires cross-training engineers and security staff in each other's domains rather than waiting to hire ready-made hybrids.

P

Packet CaptureCYBER
Recording the complete raw data crossing a network, packet by packet, for later analysis. Where flow records show that two systems communicated, packet capture shows exactly what was said — invaluable when investigating an incident or troubleshooting misbehaving integration between bridge and automation systems. Full capture generates large volumes of data, so onboard it is usually run on demand or on a rolling buffer at key points such as the IT-OT boundary.
PAM — Privileged Access ManagementCYBER
Controls dedicated to the most powerful accounts — administrators, service accounts, vendor engineers — whose credentials can change or disable entire systems. PAM solutions vault privileged passwords, issue time-limited access on request, record privileged sessions, and rotate credentials automatically after use. For fleets, PAM is particularly relevant to vendor remote access: instead of a supplier holding a permanent admin password to shipboard equipment, they check out temporary credentials for a recorded, approved session.
Passive OT Network MonitoringCYBER
Observing traffic on control networks through mirror ports or network taps, without ever transmitting onto the monitored network. Active scanning tools common in IT can crash sensitive OT devices, so passive listening is the safe way to build an asset picture and detect anomalies — new devices appearing, unexpected write commands, or a controller suddenly talking to the internet. On ships this is typically deployed on the automation and navigation networks, with alerts reviewed ashore, giving the operator visibility into OT networks that were previously a black box between dry-dockings.
Password ManagerCYBER
An application that generates strong, unique passwords and stores them in an encrypted vault unlocked by one master credential. It solves the practical problem that makes people reuse weak passwords: nobody can remember dozens of strong ones. For ships, team-oriented password managers also provide a controlled alternative to the taped-up password lists still found on some bridges and in engine control rooms, with sharing, revocation and audit built in.
Password PolicyCYBER
The organisation's rules for how passwords must be created and handled — minimum length, uniqueness, screening against known-breached passwords, and when they must be changed. Current guidance favours long passphrases and breach-checking over frequent forced changes, which mainly encourage predictable variations. A maritime password policy must also address realities like shared operational accounts, default vendor passwords on delivered equipment, and handover of credentials at crew change.
Password SprayingCYBER
An attack that tries one or two very common passwords, such as seasonal words or "Password123", against many user accounts, staying under lockout thresholds that would catch repeated guesses on a single account. Against a shipping company with hundreds of shore staff and seafarer accounts, a spray often finds at least one weak login on webmail or the VPN. Banning common passwords and requiring multi-factor authentication blunts it.
Passwordless AuthenticationCYBER
Signing in without a password at all, using instead a hardware security key, a device-bound passkey, or biometrics. Because there is no password to phish, guess or reuse, passwordless methods eliminate the most exploited weakness in most organisations. Passkeys and security keys also work without connectivity to a phone network, which makes them more practical at sea than SMS-based codes.
Patch ManagementCYBER
The controlled process of obtaining, testing and installing software updates that fix security vulnerabilities and defects. On ships this is complicated by limited bandwidth, type-approved systems that require vendor-sanctioned updates, and OT equipment that can only be patched during port stays or dry dock. A practical shipboard patch regime records the software inventory, prioritises by risk, and applies compensating controls, such as segregation, where patching is not yet possible.
PayloadCYBER
The part of an attack that does the actual damage once delivery and exploitation have succeeded, whether that is encrypting files, wiping disks, stealing data or opening a backdoor. The same delivery method, for example a phishing email to a fleet operations inbox, can carry very different payloads with very different consequences. Incident responders identify the payload to understand what an intrusion was actually meant to achieve.
Penetration TestingCYBER
An authorised, controlled attempt by security professionals to breach a ship's or company's systems in order to find weaknesses before real attackers do. Maritime penetration tests may target the vessel's networks, satellite communication equipment, remote-access paths or shore-side fleet platforms, and on OT systems they must be planned carefully to avoid disturbing live safety functions. Findings feed directly into hardening, segmentation and patching priorities, and periodic testing is increasingly expected by charterers and insurers.
Persistence (Attacker)CYBER
The methods attackers use to keep their access to a compromised system across reboots, password changes and partial clean-ups, for example scheduled tasks, added accounts or modified startup processes. Persistence is why simply removing a malicious file rarely ends an incident. On rarely audited systems, such as some onboard workstations, persistence mechanisms can survive unnoticed for a long time, so incident response should always hunt for them.
PhishingCYBER
Fraudulent messages, usually email, crafted to trick the recipient into revealing credentials, opening a malicious attachment or transferring money. Maritime variants often impersonate port agents, charterers, bunker suppliers or flag administrations, and spear phishing targets specific individuals, such as the master or a company accountant, using details harvested from AIS data and fixture reports. Because ship-shore business runs heavily on email, phishing remains the most common entry point for maritime cyber incidents, making crew and office awareness the front line of defence.
Physical Access Control (Server & Equipment Rooms)CYBER
Restricting who can physically reach the spaces and cabinets where the ship's IT and OT equipment lives — server rooms, automation cabinets, the radio room, switchboard rooms, and network racks in the ECR and on the bridge. Most OT protocols trust anyone with a cable, so physical access to a switch or controller often equals full control; locked cabinets, controlled keys, and supervision of visiting technicians are cyber controls as much as security ones. Access arrangements should mesh with the Ship Security Plan under the ISPS Code, including logging who entered technical spaces and when, particularly during port calls and yard stays.
PKI — Public Key InfrastructureCYBER
The combined system of certificate authorities, policies and software that issues, distributes and revokes digital certificates, creating a chain of trust for verifying identities and signing data. PKI underpins secure ship-shore links, signed software updates and device authentication: when an onboard system trusts an update or a connection, it is usually PKI doing the vouching. Running or consuming PKI well means protecting the issuing keys and having a working process to revoke certificates that are compromised.
PLC — Programmable Logic ControllerCYBER
A ruggedised industrial computer that reads sensors and drives actuators according to programmed logic — the workhorse of shipboard automation, found in engine control, ballast, cargo, steering, and countless auxiliary systems. PLCs run for years without attention, rarely receive firmware updates, and most accept logic changes from any engineering workstation that can reach them, often without a password. Protecting shipboard PLCs means controlling the engineering laptops and network paths that can program them, keeping verified backups of the logic, and using key switches or run-mode locks where fitted.
Port Community System SecurityCYBER
Securing the shared digital platforms through which a port's many actors — carriers, terminals, agents, customs, hauliers — exchange cargo declarations, vessel calls and release information. Because a port community system (PCS) connects so many organisations, it is both a single point of failure for the port's trade flows and an attractive target: manipulated release data has been used in real smuggling cases to extract containers. Protection combines platform hardening, strong authentication for every connected party, and monitoring for unusual transactions, since the weakest member's compromised account endangers the whole community.
Port Facility CybersecurityCYBER
The protection of port and terminal systems — terminal operating systems, cranes, gate systems, VTS interfaces and cargo data exchanges — whose disruption can stop cargo operations and affect visiting ships. In the US, MTSA-regulated facilities fall under the USCG cybersecurity rule, with requirements for plans, assessments, a designated CySO and incident reporting; in the EU, many ports are covered by NIS2 as essential entities. Ship operators are affected indirectly: a port cyber incident can delay berthing, cargo operations and clearance even when the ship itself is untouched.
Post-Quantum CryptographyCYBER
New encryption algorithms designed to withstand attack by future quantum computers, standardised by NIST from 2024 onward for global adoption. Migrating to them is a long logistical exercise — inventorying where cryptography is used, updating software, replacing embedded equipment — and shipboard and port systems with 20–30 year lifetimes make maritime one of the slower-moving sectors. The sensible starting point is a cryptographic inventory and pressure on suppliers, so that systems bought today can be upgraded rather than replaced when the transition becomes mandatory.
Power Management System SecurityCYBER
Protection of the system that starts and stops generators, synchronises them onto the switchboard, sheds load, and prevents blackout — functions that on DP vessels are directly tied to keeping position. The PMS exchanges data with the IAS, switchboards, and engine controllers, and a corrupted or manipulated PMS can trip generators or mismanage load in ways that look like ordinary electrical faults. Because blackout is one of the worst single events on a DP vessel, PMS controllers, their network segments, and their service interfaces deserve the strictest tier of access control and change management on board.
Privilege EscalationCYBER
Techniques by which an attacker who starts with a low-level foothold, such as an ordinary user account, gains higher rights, up to full administrator or domain control. With elevated privileges an intruder can disable security tools, reach fleet-wide systems and deploy ransomware everywhere at once. Limiting admin rights, patching, and separating everyday accounts from administrative ones raise the cost of escalation.
Protocol Converters & GatewaysCYBER
Small devices that translate between the many languages of shipboard equipment — NMEA serial to Ethernet, Modbus to OPC UA, CAN to IP — allowing old and new systems to interoperate. Each converter is a network bridge that can quietly connect segments intended to be separate, and many are installed by service engineers, run outdated embedded firmware, keep default passwords, and appear in no drawing. Every gateway on board should be in the OT asset inventory with a documented purpose, hardened configuration, and a clear answer to the question of what two networks it joins and why.
Purple TeamCYBER
A way of working where attackers (red team) and defenders (blue team) collaborate directly instead of competing: the red team demonstrates an attack technique, and the blue team immediately checks whether their tools and procedures detect it, tuning them on the spot. For a shipping company buying penetration testing, asking for a purple team element means the exercise improves the defence in real time, rather than just producing a report of what went undetected.

Q

Quantum Computing ThreatCYBER
The risk that sufficiently powerful quantum computers will eventually break the public-key encryption that protects today's communications, transactions and digital signatures. No such machine exists yet, but the threat is already practical through "harvest now, decrypt later": adversaries can record encrypted traffic today — including ship-shore communications and commercial data — and decrypt it once the technology matures. For data that must stay confidential for a decade or more, this makes planning the move to quantum-resistant encryption a present-day task rather than a distant one.

R

Radar System SecurityCYBER
Protection of the ship's radar installations, which today are networked computers that exchange target data, AIS overlays, and chart information with the rest of the bridge suite. Radar processors run commercial operating systems, take software updates, and share the bridge network, so they face the same malware and update-path risks as ECDIS; researchers have shown that manipulating the data feeding a radar display can move or erase targets. Keeping radar on the protected navigation network, controlling its service interfaces, and maintaining the watchkeeper's habit of cross-checking radar against visual observation and AIS are the practical defences.
RansomwareCYBER
Malware that encrypts files or locks systems and demands payment for their restoration, often combined with the threat of leaking stolen data. Shipping companies, ports and terminals have suffered major ransomware incidents that halted bookings, cargo operations and communications for days or weeks. Offline backups, network segmentation and a rehearsed incident response plan determine whether an attack becomes an inconvenience or an operational crisis; paying the ransom is discouraged and offers no guarantee of recovery.
Ransomware-as-a-ServiceCYBER
A criminal business model in which ransomware developers lease their malware and infrastructure to affiliates, who carry out attacks and share the ransom proceeds. It lowers the skill barrier dramatically, which is why mid-sized shipping companies, ports and suppliers, not just headline-grabbing giants, are routinely hit. The model also explains why attacks by the "same" ransomware brand can vary widely in competence and behaviour.
RBAC — Role-Based Access ControlCYBER
Granting permissions according to a person's role — master, chief engineer, superintendent, vendor technician — rather than configuring access individually for each user. When someone joins, changes position or signs off, their access follows the role automatically, which keeps permissions consistent and auditable across a fleet with constant crew rotation. RBAC works best when roles are reviewed periodically so they do not quietly accumulate rights they no longer need.
Recognised Security Organisation (RSO)CYBER
An organisation authorised by a flag administration or port state to perform security assessments, plan approvals and verifications on its behalf under the ISPS Code — often a classification society. As administrations bring cyber threats into ship security oversight, RSOs increasingly review cyber-related content in security assessments and plans as part of their delegated work. The delegation's scope is defined by each administration, so what an RSO may approve regarding cyber varies between flags.
ReconnaissanceCYBER
The attacker's information-gathering phase before an attack: researching the target's people, technology and public footprint. For a shipping company this includes staff lists on LinkedIn, vessel and fleet details from public trackers, exposed systems found by internet scanning, and technical details leaked in job adverts or press releases. What a company publishes about its crews, systems and schedules directly feeds later phishing and intrusion attempts.
Red TeamCYBER
Authorised specialists who simulate a real attacker to test how well an organisation's defences and people hold up — going beyond scanning for weaknesses to actually attempting intrusion, phishing and sometimes physical access. A maritime red team exercise might target the path from a phishing email in the office to remote access on a vessel, or test whether a visitor can plug into a network in the engine control room. The rules of engagement are agreed in advance so safety-critical systems are never put at risk.
Redundant Communications FailoverCYBER
Maintaining more than one independent communications path — for example VSAT, LEO satellite, 4G/5G near shore — with automatic switchover when the primary link fails or degrades. For cybersecurity, failover keeps monitoring, alerting and remote support available during an incident, and prevents a single jammed or failed link from cutting the vessel off. The failover paths must be secured to the same standard as the primary; a forgotten backup link with weaker protection is a classic way in for attackers.
Reefer Container Monitoring SecurityCYBER
Securing the systems that monitor refrigerated containers on board — power-line or wireless monitoring units, deck sockets, and the software that reports reefer temperatures to the ship and to shore logistics platforms. Reefer telemetry links the cargo system to shore networks and to thousands of third-party containers, each carrying its own controller and radio that the ship neither owns nor manages. The monitoring network should be segregated from ship control systems, and crews should recognise that a manipulated or failed monitoring feed can mask spoiling cargo worth more than the ship's freight for the voyage.
Remote Access ManagementCYBER
The governance of all connections made into a vessel's systems from ashore, whether by the company's technical department, equipment manufacturers or service providers performing remote diagnostics. Good practice includes individually authorised and time-limited sessions, multi-factor authentication, encrypted connections, activity logging, and the ability for ship's staff to see and terminate any active session. Unmanaged, always-on vendor connections to engine or DP systems are a recurring weakness found in vessel cyber assessments and are directly addressed by IACS UR E26.
Remote OEM DiagnosticsCYBER
Standing connections that let equipment manufacturers monitor and troubleshoot their systems on board — engines, automation, DP, cranes — from shore support centres. Each OEM typically installs its own gateway, VPN, or modem, so a single vessel can accumulate half a dozen third-party remote channels into its OT, each managed to a different standard and some unknown to the operator. Sound practice is to route all OEM access through one controlled, logged entry point, keep connections disabled until crew explicitly enable them for a scheduled session, and include every OEM link in the ship's cyber risk assessment.
Remote Operations Centre SecurityCYBER
Protecting the shore facilities from which vessels are monitored, supported or directly controlled — an increasingly central node as remote and autonomous operations grow. A remote operations centre (ROC) concentrates risk: whoever controls it can potentially influence every connected vessel, so its networks, operator workstations, and the authenticated links to each ship must be defended to a standard matching that consequence. Key measures include strict access control and vetting of operators, encrypted and mutually authenticated ship links, and rehearsed procedures for vessels to fall back to safe autonomous or manual states if the centre is compromised or cut off.
Removable Media PolicyCYBER
A company procedure governing the use of USB sticks, external drives and memory cards on shipboard systems, since removable media is a primary route for malware onto vessels. Typical measures include company-issued encrypted sticks only, mandatory scanning at a dedicated kiosk before use, blocking of USB ports on critical equipment, and strict rules for media brought aboard by vendors and surveyors. The policy matters most for systems that are otherwise isolated, such as ECDIS and automation stations, where a chart update or software patch on an infected stick can defeat every network defence.
Risk AppetiteCYBER
A statement from top management about how much cyber risk the company is willing to accept in pursuit of its business. It sets boundaries for practical decisions: whether vendors may have standing remote access to vessel OT, how long a known vulnerability may remain unpatched, or whether crew Wi-Fi may share any infrastructure with business systems. Without a stated appetite, these decisions get made inconsistently at low levels; with one, the risk register has something to be measured against.
Root Cause Analysis (Cyber)CYBER
Digging past the immediate trigger of an incident to find the underlying weaknesses that allowed it to happen. "A crew member clicked a phishing link" is rarely the root cause — the deeper answers may be that email filtering was weak, the account had unnecessary administrator rights, and there was no second barrier once the attacker was in. Shipping companies know this method from marine casualty investigation; applied to cyber incidents, it turns each event into fixes that prevent whole categories of recurrence.
RootkitCYBER
Malware designed to hide its own presence and that of other malicious components by embedding itself deep in the operating system, sometimes below the level normal security tools can see. An infected machine can appear completely clean while remaining under attacker control. Because rootkits are so hard to remove reliably, the standard remedy is to rebuild the system from known-good media, a significant undertaking for equipment on a vessel at sea.
RPO — Recovery Point ObjectiveCYBER
The maximum amount of data, measured in time, the company can afford to lose when restoring from backup. An RPO of 24 hours means daily backups are acceptable; an RPO of 15 minutes requires continuous replication. Different systems justify different RPOs — losing a day of planned maintenance entries is an annoyance, while losing a day of chartering and accounting transactions may be serious. RPO decisions directly determine how backup systems must be designed and paid for.
RTO — Recovery Time ObjectiveCYBER
The maximum time a system or process may be down before the disruption becomes unacceptable, as defined in the business impact analysis. The RTO drives recovery planning: a two-hour RTO for the fleet communication platform demands standby systems and rehearsed procedures, while a one-week RTO for an archive server allows simpler arrangements. Agreeing RTOs in advance forces an honest conversation between management, who set the expectations, and IT, who must be resourced to meet them.
RTU — Remote Terminal UnitCYBER
A field device that collects sensor readings and executes commands at a remote location on behalf of a central control system, communicating over serial links or networks. Aboard ship, RTU-style units gather tank levels, valve positions, and machinery data from distributed spaces and feed them to the IAS or cargo control system. They usually speak unauthenticated protocols such as Modbus and sit in unmanned spaces, so their security rests on the segmentation of the networks they use and the physical security of the compartments where they are mounted.

S

Safety Instrumented System (SIS) SecurityCYBER
Protection of the independent systems whose only job is to bring a process to a safe state — emergency shutdown of engines and fuel, fire and gas detection responses, and cargo ESD on tankers and gas carriers. The SIS is the last automatic barrier before an accident, so it must remain independent of the basic control system; cyber incidents ashore have shown attackers deliberately targeting safety controllers to disable that barrier. On board, SIS logic solvers and their engineering interfaces should be physically and logically separated from the IAS, changes strictly controlled and recorded, and any shared components between control and safety treated as a design weakness.
Safety-Security ConvergenceCYBER
The recognition that safety and cybersecurity can no longer be managed separately, because a cyber attack on a digitalised ship can cause exactly the accidents that safety management exists to prevent. It shows in regulation — IMO resolution MSC.428(98) placed cyber risk inside the ISM safety management system — and in engineering, where standards for safety-critical systems increasingly demand security analysis alongside failure analysis. Practically it means hazard identification that includes deliberate attack, and safety and security teams working from a shared risk picture instead of parallel ones.
SandboxingCYBER
Running a suspicious file or program inside an isolated, disposable environment to observe what it does before it can touch real systems. Email gateways and endpoint tools use sandboxes to detonate attachments safely, catching malware that has no known signature. The same principle applies operationally: files arriving by USB for an onboard system can be opened first on an isolated scanning station rather than directly on operational equipment.
SBOM — Software Bill of MaterialsCYBER
A structured list of the software components, libraries and versions contained in a product, comparable to a parts list for a machine. For shipboard systems, an SBOM lets owners and class societies check quickly whether a newly disclosed vulnerability affects equipment on board, instead of waiting for each vendor to investigate. Supplier documentation requirements under IACS UR E27 and product regulation such as the EU Cyber Resilience Act are pushing SBOMs into standard maritime procurement practice.
SCADACYBER
Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition — the architecture in which a central supervisory system gathers data from distributed controllers and lets operators monitor and command a process from screens. A ship's integrated automation system is essentially a floating SCADA installation: field devices around the vessel report to redundant servers, which present the plant to the crew in the ECR and on the bridge. The term matters in maritime cyber work because much published ICS security guidance is written in SCADA vocabulary, and mapping it onto shipboard automation is how those lessons get applied at sea.
SD-WAN (Maritime)CYBER
Software-defined wide area networking: technology that manages a vessel's multiple communication links — VSAT, LEO constellations, cellular — as one intelligent, encrypted network, steering each type of traffic over the best available path. Beyond performance, SD-WAN carries security benefits: consistent encryption across all links, central policy control for the whole fleet, and the ability to prioritise safety-relevant and security traffic over crew streaming. It has become the standard connectivity architecture for modern fleets.
Secrets ManagementCYBER
Securely storing and controlling the non-human credentials that systems use to talk to each other — API keys, service-account passwords, tokens and certificates. These secrets are easily leaked by being hard-coded into scripts, configuration files or onboard integration software, where they persist unnoticed for years. A secrets-management system keeps them in an encrypted vault, issues them to applications at runtime, rotates them regularly, and logs every access.
Secure BootCYBER
A hardware-backed startup check in which a computer verifies the cryptographic signature of each piece of software it loads — firmware, bootloader, operating system — and refuses to run anything unsigned or tampered with. This blocks a particularly stealthy class of attack where malware embeds itself below the operating system and survives reinstallation. As shipboard equipment is replaced and upgraded, requiring secure boot support in new bridge, automation and network hardware raises the baseline for the whole vessel.
Secure Development LifecycleCYBER
A development process in which security is designed, implemented and tested throughout a product's life rather than added afterwards — covering requirements, threat modelling, secure coding, testing, release and vulnerability handling. Standards such as IEC 62443-4-1 define such lifecycles for control system products, and class societies expect suppliers of shipboard computer-based systems to follow one when demonstrating compliance with UR E27. For buyers, evidence of a secure development lifecycle is a key question to put to bridge, automation and software vendors.
Secure Ship Reporting SystemsCYBER
Protecting the mandatory and commercial reporting flows from ships to authorities and operators — arrival notifications through maritime single windows, position reports such as LRIT, dangerous-cargo declarations, and emissions or fisheries reporting. These channels carry data that authorities and companies act on, so falsified or intercepted reports can enable sanctions evasion, smuggling or distorted compliance records, while leaked reports expose voyage patterns. Security rests on authenticating the reporting party, protecting the transmission, and cross-checking reported data against independent sources such as AIS and port records.
Security Awareness TrainingCYBER
Structured education that teaches seafarers and shore staff to recognise phishing, handle removable media safely, use strong authentication and report suspected incidents. Effective maritime programmes use ship-specific scenarios, a spoofed agent email before port arrival, an infected USB from a service technician, rather than generic office examples. Since crew behaviour is a factor in most shipboard incidents, awareness training is among the most cost-effective cyber defences a company can invest in, and it should be refreshed regularly as threats evolve.
Security by DesignCYBER
The principle that security is built into systems from the start rather than bolted on afterwards. For shipowners the key moment is newbuilding and retrofit: specifying network segregation, secure remote access and hardened configurations in the yard contract costs little, while re-engineering a delivered vessel's networks costs a great deal and may wait years for a drydock. Class societies now publish cyber notations and rules for new vessels, giving owners a concrete way to demand security by design from yards and system integrators.
Security Clauses in ContractsCYBER
Contract terms that make security obligations explicit and enforceable with suppliers, yards and service providers: requirements to protect company data, notify the company of breaches within a set time, allow security audits, patch their products, and control their own remote access. Charter parties increasingly include cyber clauses too, such as the BIMCO cyber security clause allocating responsibilities between owner and charterer. Without contractual teeth, a vendor's security promises are only marketing.
Security CultureCYBER
The shared attitudes and everyday habits that determine how people actually behave around security when nobody is checking — whether doors get held open for strangers, passwords get shared "just this once", and suspicious emails get reported or ignored. Like safety culture, it is set from the top: if managers bypass rules under commercial pressure, crews will too. A strong security culture shows up in small routine behaviours on board and in the office, and it is built through consistent leadership example, not through policy documents alone.
Security Level (IEC 62443 SL 1–4)CYBER
A graded measure in IEC 62443 of how capable an attacker a system or zone is meant to resist: SL 1 covers casual or accidental misuse, SL 2 intentional attacks with simple means, SL 3 sophisticated attacks with moderate resources, and SL 4 attacks by well-resourced, highly skilled adversaries. In shipboard practice, target security levels are assigned to zones — for example a higher level for propulsion control than for the crew welfare network — and equipment and countermeasures are selected to achieve them. Class rules and UR E26/E27 draw on this concept when setting requirements for onboard systems.
Security Metrics & KPIsCYBER
Measurements that show whether the security programme is actually working, reported to management alongside safety and commercial figures. Useful maritime examples include the percentage of vessels with up-to-date patches, time taken to remove leavers' accounts, phishing test failure rates by fleet, and drill completion rates. Good metrics track things the company can act on; counting "millions of blocked attacks" impresses nobody and informs nothing.
Security Operations Centre (SOC)CYBER
A team, in-house or outsourced, that monitors security alerts around the clock, investigates suspicious activity and coordinates response. A maritime SOC receives logs and alerts from vessels over their satellite links and watches for signs of compromise across the fleet, from malware detections on bridge PCs to unusual traffic on OT gateways. For shipping companies without their own security staff, subscribing to a managed SOC service is a common way to gain continuous monitoring capability.
Security PolicyCYBER
The top-level document in which management states the company's security objectives, assigns responsibilities and commits resources — the cyber equivalent of the safety and environmental policy required by the ISM Code. It is deliberately short and stable, with detailed procedures (passwords, remote access, media handling) kept in supporting documents that can change without re-issuing the policy. Its real function is to make security a stated management commitment that auditors, crews and vetting inspectors can hold the company to.
Security Test Reports (FAT/SAT Cyber Testing)CYBER
Documented results of cyber security testing performed at the Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) and Ship/Site Acceptance Test (SAT) stages of a newbuild or retrofit. Tests verify that required security functions — segmentation, access control, logging, removal of default passwords, malware protection — actually work in the delivered configuration, first at the supplier's works and then installed on board. IACS UR E26/E27 and class cyber notations expect such test evidence, and the reports become part of the vessel's handover documentation.
Segregation of DutiesCYBER
Splitting critical tasks between different people so no single person can carry out and conceal a damaging action alone — the same logic as requiring two signatures on a payment. In practice this means the person who approves a payment is not the one who registers the supplier, and the administrator who changes systems is not the one who audits the logs. It is a key defence against both insider misuse and the invoice-fraud attacks that regularly hit shipping companies, where a compromised email account asks accounts payable to change a supplier's bank details.
Serial Communications (Maritime OT)CYBER
The RS-232, RS-422, and RS-485 point-to-point and multidrop links that still carry much of a ship's critical data — NMEA sentences between bridge instruments, Modbus between controllers, and interfaces to VDR and automation. Serial links are often assumed safe because they are not "networked", but they carry no authentication, and the serial-to-Ethernet converters increasingly used to extend them quietly expose these trusting connections to IP networks. Treating serial infrastructure as part of the attack surface — knowing where every line runs, what converters touch it, and what could inject data onto it — closes a blind spot common on older tonnage.
Session HijackingCYBER
Taking over a user's authenticated session with a website or application, typically by stealing the session token or cookie, so the attacker is treated as the already-logged-in user. It can bypass even multi-factor authentication, since the login already happened. A hijacked session on a fleet management portal or webmail gives the attacker everything that user can see and do until the session is revoked.
Session ManagementCYBER
The handling of a user's authenticated "session" — the period between logging in and logging out — including the tokens that keep them signed in, timeouts for inactivity, and clean termination when access should end. Weak session management lets attackers hijack a logged-in session and act as the user without ever knowing the password. On ships it has a physical dimension: workstations on the bridge or in the engine control room are accessible to many people, so sessions left open indefinitely quietly defeat whatever login controls exist.
Shadow ITCYBER
Hardware, software and cloud services used for company work without the IT department's knowledge or approval — a private file-sharing account for exchanging stability files, a messaging app group for fleet operations, or an unauthorised 4G router installed on board for convenience. Shadow IT is usually adopted with good intentions, because official tools are slow or missing, but it bypasses backups, access control and monitoring. The fix combines discovery, providing decent official alternatives, and clear rules about what is off-limits.
Ship-Shore Interface RiskCYBER
The cyber exposure created wherever vessel systems exchange data with the shore: satellite links, port Wi-Fi, cargo and stowage file transfers, electronic pre-arrival reporting, and connections to terminal or agency systems. Each interface is a potential entry point in both directions, since a compromised ship can also threaten port systems and vice versa. Managing this risk means knowing every interface that exists, securing each with encryption and authentication, and treating files received from third parties, such as stowage plans, with the same caution as email attachments.
Shipboard CCTV & IP Camera SecurityCYBER
Securing the network cameras and recorders used for engine room monitoring, mooring deck views, security surveillance, and increasingly for remote operations support. IP cameras are notorious for default passwords, unpatched firmware, and known exploits, and on ships they are often plugged into whichever network was convenient during installation, creating stepping stones between segments. Cameras and recorders should live on their own VLAN, use changed credentials and maintained firmware, and any shore streaming of camera feeds should pass through the ship's controlled internet gateway rather than a vendor's own uplink.
Shipboard Cyber DrillCYBER TRAINING
A practical exercise conducted on board, in the style of fire and abandon-ship drills, where the crew responds to a simulated cyber scenario — for example ECDIS showing a false position, loss of the automation network, or a ransomware message on the ship's business computer. The drill tests whether the crew can recognise the event, switch to backup procedures, isolate affected systems and report correctly to the company. Drills are logged like other ISM drills and their findings feed the lessons learned process.
SIEMCYBER
Security Information and Event Management: a platform that collects logs from many sources, servers, firewalls, endpoints and OT gateways, correlates them and raises alerts when patterns suggest an attack. In a fleet context, a SIEM aggregates events from vessels and shore offices so that analysts can spot, for example, the same suspicious login attempts appearing across several ships. Bandwidth constraints mean shipboard log collection must be selective, forwarding security-relevant events rather than raw data streams.
Silent Cyber & War Exclusion ClausesCYBER REG
Two insurance pitfalls every shipowner should understand. "Silent cyber" refers to traditional policies that neither clearly cover nor clearly exclude cyber losses, leaving owners uncertain whether a cyber-caused casualty is insured; regulators have pushed insurers to remove this ambiguity, often by adding explicit exclusions such as the marine cyber clauses LMA 5402 and 5403. War exclusion clauses add a further gap: if a cyber attack is attributed to a state actor, insurers may treat it as an act of war and decline the claim. Owners should review exactly how their hull, P&I and cyber policies interact before an incident, not after.
SmishingCYBER
Phishing conducted over SMS or messaging apps, usually a short urgent text with a malicious link, posing as a bank, delivery service, or the employer's IT department. Seafarers are reachable this way even when email is filtered, and small phone screens make fake links harder to spot. Messages claiming crew payroll or travel bookings need urgent action are typical maritime lures.
SMS Integration of Cyber (ISM)CYBER REG
Incorporating cyber risk management into the company's Safety Management System under the ISM Code, as called for by IMO Resolution MSC.428(98) from the first annual Document of Compliance verification after 1 January 2021. In practice this means the SMS contains cyber risk assessments, procedures for critical systems, incident reporting routes, drills and training — all subject to the same audits, non-conformity handling and management review as other SMS elements. Flag and class auditors now routinely check for it, so cyber that lives only in the IT department is both a security gap and a compliance finding.
SOARCYBER
Security Orchestration, Automation and Response: a platform that turns routine security procedures into automated playbooks — for example, when a phishing email is reported, automatically checking the link, searching for other recipients, and quarantining matching messages. SOAR reduces response times from hours to minutes and frees scarce analysts for judgement calls. For maritime operators, playbooks can encode fleet-specific steps, such as notifying the DPA or isolating a vessel's business network while keeping operational links up.
Social EngineeringCYBER
The manipulation of people, rather than technology, to gain access or information, exploiting trust, urgency and authority. Maritime examples include callers impersonating the DPA or a vendor engineer to extract passwords, fake port officials requesting crew and cargo details, and fraudulent payment-change requests sent to the company's accounts team. Phishing is its most common form, but social engineering also happens by phone, in person at the gangway, and through social media, so procedures for verifying identities and payment changes are essential.
Software Registry (UR E27)CYBER
An inventory of the software installed in a vessel's computer-based systems, maintained as part of the supplier documentation required under IACS UR E27 for ships contracted from 1 July 2024. It records what software and versions run on which systems, supporting change control, patch decisions and vulnerability checks throughout the ship's life. Keeping the registry current after service visits and updates is an operational duty — an outdated registry undermines every assessment built on it.
Spear PhishingCYBER
Phishing aimed at a specific person or small group, using researched detail, real names, ongoing voyages, actual counterparties, to make the message convincing. An operations manager might receive a mail referencing a genuine port call and asking to open an "updated stowage plan". Because it exploits accurate context rather than generic bait, technical filters catch less of it and trained scepticism matters more.
SpywareCYBER
Software that covertly monitors a user or system, collecting information such as browsing activity, messages, credentials or screenshots, and sending it to a third party. In a commercial shipping context, spyware on an executive's or chartering manager's device can leak negotiating positions and voyage plans. On mobile phones it is also a known tool for surveilling individuals of interest, including personnel in sensitive trades.
SQL InjectionCYBER
An attack that abuses poorly written web applications by entering database commands into input fields, tricking the application into running them. A successful injection against a booking portal or crewing system can expose or alter entire databases, including customer, cargo and personal data. It is one of the oldest web vulnerabilities and remains common; the fix lies in secure coding practices, which is a key question to put to software suppliers.
SSO — Single Sign-OnCYBER
Letting users authenticate once and then access many applications without separate logins for each. SSO improves security as well as convenience: authentication is concentrated in one well-protected identity service where multi-factor authentication and monitoring can be enforced, and departing employees or crew lose access to everything by disabling a single account. The trade-off is that the identity service becomes critical infrastructure and must be protected and made resilient accordingly.
State-Sponsored Threat ActorsCYBER
Attack groups funded or directed by national governments, pursuing espionage, pre-positioning for disruption, or sanctions evasion rather than quick profit. Maritime transport is strategic infrastructure, so ports, shipping lines, shipbuilders and navigation systems attract this class of attacker, and interest rises sharply during geopolitical tension. They are the most capable and patient adversaries a shipping company can face, though their tools often trickle down to ordinary criminals over time.
Statement of Compliance (Cyber)CYBER
A document issued by a classification society or other assurance body confirming that a ship, company or product has been verified against a defined set of cyber security requirements — often used where a full class notation or certification is not sought. Statements of compliance are commonly requested by charterers, insurers or flag administrations as portable evidence of a completed assessment. The value of the document depends entirely on its stated scope and reference standard, which should always be read, not assumed.
Steering Gear Control System SecurityCYBER
Protection of the control chain from bridge wheel and autopilot down to the steering gear hydraulics — follow-up amplifiers, rudder feedback, and the interfaces linking autopilot, IBS, and steering controllers. As steering controls move from hardwired circuits to networked electronics and software-based autopilots, the rudder becomes reachable through the same bridge networks and update paths as everything else. Defences include keeping steering control interfaces on the protected navigation network, controlling autopilot software updates, and preserving the crew's proficiency in non-follow-up and emergency steering so a compromised control chain never means a lost rudder.
Supplier Cyber AssuranceCYBER
The process of verifying that vendors and service providers — equipment makers, software houses, remote maintenance providers, agents — meet defined cyber security expectations before and during their engagement. In shipping this includes reviewing supplier documentation required under IACS UR E27, controlling remote access to onboard systems, and including security clauses in service agreements. Many maritime incidents originate in the supply chain, so vetting suppliers is treated as part of the company's own risk management, not a courtesy.
Supply Chain AttackCYBER
An attack that reaches its target indirectly by compromising a trusted supplier, for example tampering with a software update, a vendor's remote-access tool or equipment delivered to the ship. Vessels depend on many third parties, chart providers, automation vendors, communication providers and managers, and each trusted update channel is a potential vector. Defences include vetting suppliers' security practices, verifying the integrity of updates before installation and contractual security requirements, obligations that NIS2 and IACS UR E27 now make explicit for much of the industry.

T

Tabletop ExerciseCYBER TRAINING
A discussion-based exercise where key people walk through a simulated incident scenario around a table, step by step, without touching real systems. A facilitator presents developments — "the crewing system is encrypted; the attackers demand payment; a journalist is calling" — and participants explain what they would actually do, exposing gaps in plans, unclear responsibilities and missing contact details. Tabletops are cheap, safe and revealing, and work best when they include management, DPA, IT, operations and communications people together, not IT alone.
Terminal Operating System SecurityCYBER
Securing the terminal operating system (TOS) — the software that orchestrates a container or cargo terminal, planning vessel stowage, yard positions, gate moves and equipment assignments. A TOS outage effectively stops the terminal, as major incidents at global operators have demonstrated, and manipulated TOS data can be used to misdirect or steal specific containers. Defences focus on separating the TOS from office networks, controlling the many interfaces it has with lines, customs and equipment, and maintaining tested procedures for running essential operations while the system is down.
Third-Party Risk ManagementCYBER
The ongoing discipline of identifying and controlling the cyber risks that suppliers and partners bring with them — equipment vendors with remote access to vessels, software providers hosting company data, manning agents, agents and yards. Attackers increasingly reach targets through their suppliers, so a shipping company's security is capped by that of its most trusted third parties. The programme typically combines an inventory of third parties and their access, risk-based security assessments, contractual requirements and periodic review, with the deepest scrutiny reserved for those who can touch vessel OT.
Threat HuntingCYBER
Proactively searching networks and systems for signs of intrusion that automated tools have not flagged, driven by hypotheses about how an attacker might operate. Rather than waiting for alerts, a hunter might ask "if someone had a foothold on our vessel remote-access path, what traces would they leave?" and go looking. Hunting assumes compromise is possible despite defences, and regularly uncovers long-dwelling intrusions — relevant in maritime, where OT footholds can sit quietly for months.
Threat IntelligenceCYBER
Curated information about current attackers, their methods and indicators of compromise, used to anticipate and detect attacks rather than merely react to them. Maritime-specific intelligence covers campaigns targeting shipping companies, ports and offshore operators, including phishing themes tied to chartering and port calls. Companies consume it through industry bodies, commercial feeds and information-sharing communities, and feed it into their SIEM and SOC processes so that fleet defences reflect the threats actually facing shipping.
TLSCYBER
Transport Layer Security: the standard protocol that encrypts data in transit and verifies, via digital certificates, that you are connected to the genuine counterpart. It is what the padlock in a browser represents, and it protects everything from webmail to ship-shore reporting links and cloud-connected vessel performance systems. Older versions (SSL, TLS 1.0/1.1) are broken and should be disabled — a common finding on legacy shipboard equipment that has never been reconfigured.
TMSA Cybersecurity ElementCYBER
The part of OCIMF's Tanker Management and Self Assessment programme in which tanker operators rate the maturity of their cyber security management against defined key performance indicators. Companies self-assess their policies, assessments, training and incident preparedness at progressive achievement levels, and oil majors review the results during vetting. Because TMSA scores influence chartering decisions, the cybersecurity element gives tanker operators a direct commercial incentive to mature their cyber programme.
TrojanCYBER
Malware disguised as legitimate software, relying on the user to install it, for example a fake update, a cracked application, or a useful-looking utility attached to an email. Unlike a worm it does not spread by itself. On ships, trojans commonly arrive via USB sticks, downloads on crew devices, or unofficial software installed to solve a practical problem, which is why software installation on operational systems should be controlled.
Type Approval & Cyber Security (Wheelmark)CYBER
The relationship between regulatory equipment approval — such as the EU's Wheelmark under the Marine Equipment Directive — and actual cyber security. Type approval verifies that equipment meets performance and safety standards at the time of testing; historically it said little about cyber security, so an approved ECDIS or radar can still run outdated software with known vulnerabilities. Newer type-approval and class requirements increasingly include cyber criteria for computer-based systems, but crews and superintendents should understand that an approval mark is a starting point, not evidence that a device is secure as installed and maintained on their ship.
TyposquattingCYBER
Registering domain names that closely resemble a legitimate one, differing by a swapped letter or alternate ending, to catch mistyped addresses or lend credibility to fraudulent email. In shipping, a lookalike domain of a company, bunker supplier or agent is a standard ingredient of payment fraud, making a fake invoice email appear genuine at a glance. Companies often register obvious variants of their own name and train staff to check sender domains character by character.

U

UEBA — User and Entity Behaviour AnalyticsCYBER
Analytics that learn the normal behaviour of each user and device, then flag meaningful deviations — a superintendent's account logging in from an unusual country, a vessel workstation suddenly transferring gigabytes ashore at night. Because it detects abnormality rather than known attack signatures, UEBA can catch compromised accounts and insider misuse that rule-based tools miss. It typically feeds its risk scores into the SIEM or detection platform rather than acting alone.
Uncrewed Systems Link SecurityCYBER
Protecting the command-and-control links of uncrewed surface vessels (USVs), remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) and drones used in maritime operations, where the radio or acoustic link is the only way to control the platform. Threats include jamming that severs control, interception of video and sensor feeds, and spoofed commands that redirect or capture the vehicle. Sound designs encrypt and mutually authenticate the link, define safe automatic behaviour on link loss — hold station, surface, or return home — and avoid relying on a single frequency or communication path.
Unidirectional vs Bidirectional Ship-Shore Data FlowsCYBER
A basic design question for every ship-shore connection: does data only leave the vessel, or can the shore side also send data and commands back on board? Reporting, condition monitoring, and voyage data usually need only outbound flow, which can be enforced with data diodes or strict firewall rules and carries far less risk than a two-way channel. Bidirectional flows — remote access, software updates, remote-controlled functions — are sometimes necessary, but each one should be a deliberate, documented decision with authentication, logging, and crew awareness, never the default setting of a vendor gateway.
USB Charging & Media Ports on Bridge EquipmentCYBER
The USB sockets on ECDIS, radar, VDR, GMDSS, and automation consoles — fitted for chart updates and data export, but routinely used by crew to charge phones. A phone is a computer, and plugging one into an ECDIS creates a data connection to navigation-critical equipment; incidents have been traced to exactly this habit. Countermeasures are simple and cheap: provide dedicated charging points away from consoles, physically block or lock unused ports on bridge equipment, disable USB autorun functions, and reserve the remaining ports for controlled, scanned media under the ship's removable media procedures.
USCG Cybersecurity Rule (Marine Transportation System)CYBER
The US Coast Guard final rule "Cybersecurity in the Marine Transportation System", published 17 January 2025 and effective 16 July 2025. It requires US-flagged vessels and MTSA-regulated facilities to report reportable cyber incidents to the National Response Center, designate a Cybersecurity Officer (CySO), and implement cybersecurity plans, assessments, training and drills on a phased timeline. It is one of the first binding national cyber regimes for shipping and a likely model for other administrations, so non-US operators trading to the US should follow its implementation closely.

V

VDR SecurityCYBER
Protection of the Voyage Data Recorder, the "black box" that continuously captures bridge audio, radar images, position, and sensor data for accident investigation. The VDR connects to nearly every bridge system to do its job, which makes it both a useful network vantage point for an attacker and a target for anyone wanting to erase or falsify evidence after an incident; published vulnerabilities have shown recorded data can be remotely wiped on some models. VDR security means keeping its firmware maintained, restricting its network and service interfaces, and preserving data promptly and verifiably after any incident, cyber or otherwise.
Vendor Security AssessmentCYBER
The evaluation of an individual supplier's security before granting them access or buying their product — usually through questionnaires, review of certifications such as ISO 27001, evidence of testing, and for critical vendors, audits or technical review. For maritime suppliers the assessment should ask pointed, practical questions: how is remote access to vessels secured and logged, how quickly are vulnerabilities in the product patched, and what happens to company data if the relationship ends. Assessments should be repeated periodically, since a vendor's security posture can degrade after the contract is signed.
VishingCYBER
Voice phishing: fraud conducted by phone call, with the caller impersonating IT support, a bank, a port authority or a senior manager to extract credentials or trigger actions. A typical scenario is a call to a duty officer or accounts clerk citing an urgent operational problem that requires a password or an immediate payment. Caller ID can be spoofed, so identity should be verified by calling back on a known number.
VLANCYBER
Virtual Local Area Network: a way of splitting one physical network into several logically separate ones, so crew Wi-Fi, business systems and OT equipment can share the same switches and cabling while remaining isolated from each other. VLANs are the everyday building block of shipboard network segmentation, saving weight and cabling compared with fully separate physical networks. They provide logical, not physical, separation — so switch configuration must be controlled and traffic between VLANs forced through a firewall.
VPN (Virtual Private Network)CYBER
A technology that creates an encrypted tunnel between two points over an untrusted network, commonly used to link a vessel's business network to the company's shore infrastructure across the satellite connection. VPNs protect ship-shore traffic from interception and give remote engineers a controlled path into designated systems. A VPN secures the connection but not the endpoints: a compromised laptop connecting through a VPN carries its infection straight into the trusted network, so VPN access should be combined with MFA, endpoint checks and least-privilege rules.
VSAT / Satellite Communications SecurityCYBER
The protection of a vessel's satellite communication systems, VSAT terminals, L-band backups and newer low-earth-orbit services, which form the ship's permanent connection to the internet and to shore. Common weaknesses include terminals with default administrator passwords, management interfaces exposed to the internet and flat networks behind the router that give every onboard system the same exposure. Because the satellite link is both the ship's lifeline and its main attack path, terminals should be hardened and patched, management access restricted, and traffic separated so crew, business and OT data do not share one unsegmented connection.
VTS CybersecurityCYBER
Protecting vessel traffic services — the shore centres that monitor and organise ship movements in ports and congested waters using radar, AIS, CCTV and radio. A compromised VTS could feed operators a false traffic picture or broadcast misleading instructions, raising collision and grounding risk in exactly the waters where margins are smallest. Security work combines hardening the sensor and communication chains, guarding against false data injection such as spoofed AIS targets, and training operators to recognise when the picture on screen disagrees with reality.
Vulnerability AssessmentCYBER
A systematic review of a vessel's or fleet's systems to identify and rank security weaknesses, using scanning tools, configuration reviews and documentation checks. Unlike a penetration test, it does not attempt to exploit the weaknesses found, which makes it safer to perform on live shipboard OT, where active scanning must in any case be used with caution. Regular assessments give the company a prioritised worklist for patching, hardening and segmentation, and provide evidence of due diligence for class, insurers and SMS audits.
Vulnerability ScannerCYBER
A tool that automatically probes systems and compares what it finds — software versions, open services, configurations — against databases of known weaknesses, producing a prioritised list of what needs fixing. Regular scanning turns vulnerability management from guesswork into measurement. Caution is required on vessel OT networks: active scanning can crash sensitive controllers, so OT environments are usually assessed with passive monitoring or carefully scoped scans during planned downtime.

W

Watering-Hole AttackCYBER
An attack that compromises a website the intended victims are known to visit, then uses it to infect them, rather than approaching the targets directly. For the maritime sector that could be an industry news site, a port community portal or a specialised supplier's download page frequented by fleet technical staff. It is favoured by patient, targeted attackers because victims infect themselves through entirely normal browsing.
Web Content FilteringCYBER
Controlling which websites and categories of web content users can reach, blocking known-malicious sites and enforcing acceptable-use policy. On vessels it serves a double purpose: reducing the risk of drive-by malware and phishing on crew and business networks, and managing scarce satellite bandwidth by restricting high-volume or inappropriate content. Filtering is typically enforced at the firewall, the SD-WAN edge, or via DNS.
WhalingCYBER
Spear phishing aimed at the biggest targets: C-level executives, owners and senior managers, whose accounts and authority are worth the extra effort. A compromised CEO or CFO mailbox in a shipping company is a springboard for large payment fraud and gives access to the most sensitive commercial information. Senior leaders often have the widest access and the least security oversight, a combination attackers exploit deliberately.
Wiper MalwareCYBER
Malware built to destroy data and render systems unusable, sometimes disguised as ransomware even though no payment can undo the damage. The canonical maritime example is the 2017 NotPetya attack, which spread into Maersk's global network, forced the rebuild of thousands of servers and PCs, disrupted terminals and bookings worldwide, and cost the company a reported USD 250-300 million. Wipers show why offline, tested backups and network segmentation are existential controls for a shipping company, not paperwork.
WormCYBER
Malware that spreads by itself from system to system across a network, without needing users to open or run anything. Worms move fast and indiscriminately, which is how a single infected machine can compromise an entire flat, unsegmented network, including poorly separated vessel networks reached over the business LAN or by USB. Segmentation and prompt patching of the vulnerabilities worms exploit are the main defences.

X

XDRCYBER
Extended Detection and Response: a platform that correlates security signals from many sources — endpoints, email, network, cloud and identity systems — into unified incidents, rather than leaving each tool to alert in isolation. Where EDR watches individual machines, XDR can connect a phishing email ashore, a suspicious login and unusual traffic towards a vessel into one attack story. For lean maritime IT teams, the appeal is fewer consoles and faster, more complete investigations.

Z

Zero TrustCYBER
A security model that abandons the idea of a trusted internal network: every user, device and connection must be verified continuously, regardless of whether it originates on board or ashore. Applied to shipping, zero trust means a laptop on the vessel's business network gets no automatic access to OT systems, and every remote session to the ship is authenticated, authorised and logged as if it came from the open internet. Full zero-trust architectures are demanding for legacy shipboard environments, but the principle of never trusting by network location guides sensible modernisation of fleet IT and OT.
Zero-Click AttackCYBER
An attack that compromises a device with no user interaction at all, typically by exploiting a flaw in how the device automatically processes incoming data such as messages or media previews. Since there is no bad link to avoid, user training cannot prevent it; keeping devices updated and limiting what sensitive roles do on exposed devices are the practical mitigations. These exploits are expensive and mostly used by well-resourced actors against selected targets, such as executives in strategically interesting trades.
Zero-Day VulnerabilityCYBER
A software flaw that is exploited by attackers before the vendor knows about it or has released a fix, leaving defenders zero days of warning. Since no patch exists, protection depends on layered defences: segmentation to contain the compromise, application whitelisting to stop unknown code, monitoring to detect unusual behaviour, and backups to recover. For shipboard systems that already run behind on patches, the zero-day threat reinforces why architecture and containment matter more than chasing individual updates.
Zones and Conduits (IEC 62443)CYBER
The IEC 62443 method of dividing a control system into zones — groups of assets with common security requirements — connected only through defined conduits where traffic can be controlled and monitored. On a ship this typically means separating navigation, machinery automation, cargo systems, administrative IT and crew networks, with firewalls or gateways as the conduits between them. The model underpins class cyber rules and UR E26 network segmentation requirements, and a current zones-and-conduits drawing is a standard deliverable in newbuild and notation projects.
